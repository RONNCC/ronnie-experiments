00 / No background required

A chip is a machine, not a program waiting to run.

Bottom line: A chip has many tiny circuits working at once. Before manufacturing it, engineers simulate a description of those circuits and look for situations where behavior contradicts the requirements. A manufactured logic mistake is much harder to patch than a web service.

1 · Describe the machine

A chip is a piece of semiconductor material containing electronic circuits. A transistor is an electrically controlled switch; circuits combine switches to compute and store bits (0 or 1). An ASIC, application-specific integrated circuit, is a chip designed for particular jobs.

2 · Run a virtual version

RTL, register-transfer level, describes stored values and the logic that moves or transforms them. A register holds bits; a clock provides repeated timing edges. Simulation executes a time-based model of the RTL, not a physical chip. SystemVerilog is a language used for this description and for tests.

3 · Commit to matter

Tape-out means delivering the final manufacturing design files. A foundry fabricates the chip using patterned layers. A respin is a revised manufacturing run after a design change. New masks (manufacturing templates), fabrication, packaging, and validation can mean millions of dollars and months—not a quick redeploy.

A specification (spec) states intended behavior. Design verification (DV) asks whether the design satisfies that intent. Physical design turns logic into a layout; electrical timing and manufacturing checks are separate from the functional labs here. A chip may pass every simulated test and still be wrong: the tests might never ask the right question.

Why the bottleneck grows

Concurrency is the trap. Two blocks can each work alone yet fail when a queue fills during a reset while a receiver stalls. Even a 32-bit register has over four billion possible values. Add history, many registers, power states, and timing interactions, and exhaustive simulation becomes infeasible. Verification engineers build evidence, not a promise that every possible behavior was simulated.

Reported “Roughly half or more” is a commonly cited industry figure, not a universal constant. SemiWiki’s January 2026 coverage says “as much as 70%” of a design cycle can be verification [6]. Effort (person-hours) and schedule (elapsed time) are not interchangeable. This guide does not claim every project spends 70% of either.

Reported Semiconductor Engineering quotes mask-set costs of $3–5 million at 5/7 nm in its mature-node discussion [7]; a process node is a manufacturing technology generation, not a literal measurement of every transistor. A respin’s total cost and delay depend on changed layers, node, foundry capacity, and scope. “Millions and months” describes the risk of complex chips, not a fixed invoice.

Software analogy—with an important limit

RTL is closer to describing a concurrent state machine than writing a sequential function. A state machine is a system whose next stored state depends on its current state and inputs. Simulation is like testing that machine on a virtual clock. Unlike a normal software binary, the hardware’s state storage, connections, and parallel operations become physical structures. Some silicon bugs have firmware workarounds (changes to embedded software); others require a new chip.

01 / The engineer’s day

Turn a pile of failures into actionable evidence.

Bottom line: A verification engineer builds a controlled world around the chip, sends legal and stressful inputs, checks the results, and investigates discrepancies. The hard part is often deciding which failures share a cause and whether the design, test, or requirement is wrong.

The test machinery

A testbench is the simulated environment around the design under test (DUT). A driver applies inputs, a monitor observes transfers, and a scoreboard compares results with expected behavior. A reference model independently computes the expected result; it can be wrong too.

UVM, Universal Verification Methodology, is a standardized SystemVerilog class library and methodology for reusable testbenches—not a simulator, language, or proof system. Its sequences describe transactions (meaningful operations such as sending a packet), drivers turn them into signal activity, and monitors publish observations. [8]

Constrained-random testing generates randomized inputs subject to rules, such as legal packet lengths. A seed initializes the generator so a failure can be reproduced with the same tool/version/configuration. Random does not mean uniformly sampling every possible situation.

Checks versus reach

An assertion is an executable property, such as “a stalled sender must keep its offered word stable.” Functional coverage records whether planned behaviors occurred. A bin is a bucket for a behavior, e.g. reset while full. A cross records combinations of categories. Code coverage measures exercised RTL structures, such as branches; it does not prove requirements were checked.

A regression runs many tests, seeds, and configurations, often overnight on a compute farm. A failure is a violated check, mismatch, crash, or timeout—not automatically an RTL bug. Triage groups failures, assesses severity, and assigns investigation. Coverage closure means resolving gaps against a reviewed plan, including justified exclusions for impossible cases—not blindly making a gauge read 100%.

Life of a failing regression

  1. 18:00 · Freeze the inputs. Record source commit, tool version, test, seed, constraints, and configuration.
  2. 02:13 · A check fires. Save the first meaningful error and a waveform: signal values over simulated time. A timeout may be only a late symptom.
  3. 08:30 · Deduplicate. Cluster by meaningful signature and recent changes; 800 failed tests may be one shared problem.
  4. 09:00 · Reproduce and narrow. Replay the same run. Look backward from the error to the first wrong transition, comparing spec, RTL, and testbench.
  5. 10:00 · Assign with evidence. File a ticket with the minimal trace, causal hypothesis, ownership, and uncertainty. A testbench race (ordering-dependent behavior) can masquerade as bad RTL.
  6. Later · Fix and prevent recurrence. Review the patch, rerun the failing seed, add a focused test, then run broader regressions. Update coverage and the debug playbook.

02 / Interactive lab A · Teaching model

Debug the failure.

Bottom line: Find the first broken contract, not just the final error. These three generated traces come from the state updates shown in the code, not screenshots or Bronco runs.

A valid/ready handshake transfers a word only when both signals are 1 at a rising clock edge. Valid means “this word is offered”; ready means “the receiver can accept it.” When valid=1 and ready=0 (backpressure), the sender must hold valid and data until acceptance, unless reset intervenes. Our one-entry buffer may accept and emit at the same edge. rst_n is a synchronous active-low reset: 0 resets at the edge. <= is a nonblocking assignment: right-hand sides use pre-edge state; registered updates appear afterward.

Predict first: which contract was broken? Toggle signal visibility with the buttons. Click the plot or use the time slider. Rows are sampled immediately before each rising edge; clk is implicit, one edge per column. Data is decimal. Inactive data has no meaning. Traces begin in a known empty state after a preceding drain edge (in_valid=0, out_ready=1); this avoids assuming power-up initialization, including in the broken-reset case.

03 / Interactive lab B · Toy model, not Bronco data
Close coverage. Meet the long tail.
Bottom line: More random tests quickly hit easy behaviors, then repeatedly revisit them. Targeting a specific missing behavior can help—but first check that it is reachable, legal, and measured correctly.
Predict which strategy reaches the last 16 dashed bins first. These represent rare combinations. Then move the sliders. A filled square means a planned behavior occurred, not that the design passed a correctness check.
Model assumptions and why this is not a benchmark
There are 48 reachable bins, 32 common and 16 rare. A bin is hit when test count × its strategy-specific rate exceeds a fixed deterministic threshold. Random common/rare rates are 0.16 and 0.012/difficulty; human rates are 0.13 and 0.038/difficulty; agent rates are 0.14 and 0.085/difficulty. The agent’s advantage is deliberately assumed, not measured. Curves are cumulative counts, not statistical uncertainty ranges.
All strategies receive the same number of tests, not the same engineering time, compute, license cost, or model inference budget. Real agents can choose illegal or redundant stimulus, hit unreachable bins, or optimize a misleading coverage plan. Test design, execution, and checking still matter. A stronger real evaluation compares reachable-bin gain and new validated bugs per total cost with held-out designs and reviewed exclusions.



04 / Interactive lab C
From a playbook to an agent.
Bottom line: The useful agent does not merely summarize an error. It follows a debug process, uses tools to gather bounded evidence, and hands a human a defensible conclusion—or an explicit “not enough evidence.”
A playbook is a repeatable investigation procedure. An agent is a model-driven system that selects actions and tools over multiple steps. A large language model (LLM) predicts and generates text; giving it tools does not make its conclusions correct. Skills map · study in this order
First · hardware intuition
Clocked state updates, queues, reset, backpressure, and assertions. Read a tiny waveform and explain what transferred. Learn to separate pre-edge samples from post-edge state.
Next · Python / C++ + full-stack
Python for orchestration and parsers; C++ where profiling proves data access needs it. Build a run browser linking errors to time windows and source lines. Treat artifact access and audit logs as product features.
Then · agentic LLM systems
Typed tool contracts, retrieval grounded in artifact IDs, hypothesis tracking, model/version evaluation, safe retries, and human escalation. Learn computer architecture through memory, pipelines (staged processing), and arbitration (choosing among competing requests).

07 / Practice speaking with evidence
Twenty questions. Explain the choice, then the trade-off.
Bottom line: Strong answers connect models to reliable tools, evidence, safe deployment, and the engineer’s actual workflow. These are original practice prompts and illustrative answers—not Bronco’s interview questions or architecture.
Ten smart questions to ask the founders
  1. Which failure classes are strongest today, and where does the agent deliberately abstain?
  2. How do you measure net engineer time saved after ticket review and correction?
  3. How are evaluations split across customers, designs, versions, and previously seen bugs?
  4. What does a successful small-block pilot need to demonstrate before expansion?
  5. How has the playbook/library approach changed as agents become more goal-driven?
  6. Which waveform access or simulator integration bottleneck most limits product capability?
  7. What data can leave a customer boundary, and who approves enterprise-model endpoints?
  8. How do engineers share reusable debugging methods without leaking customer design IP (intellectual property)?
  9. For this role, how much time is core systems versus UI versus onsite integration—and how often is travel expected?
  10. What do “run towards the fire” and after-hours ownership mean in practice, including support rotation and recovery time?
