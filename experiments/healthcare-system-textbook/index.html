CHAPTER 01 · FOUNDATION

The big picture: who provides, who pays, who regulates

Three questions unlock the whole system: who provides care, who pays for it, and who writes the rules. Every visit and every dollar in this book travels between those three groups.

The three jobs

  • Providers deliver care: hospitals operate beds and operating rooms; physician practices handle office visits; urgent care centers treat same-day problems; emergency medical services (EMS) respond to 911 calls; pharmacies, laboratories and imaging centers support diagnosis and treatment.
  • Payers finance care: insurers and government programs collect premiums or taxes, then pay providers negotiated amounts when care is used.
  • Regulators set floor rules: federal agencies write the rules for Medicare, Medicaid and insurance markets; states license clinicians, hospitals and insurers; the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) runs Medicare and the federal marketplace and sets fraud and quality rules.

Where the money comes from

National health spending in the United States runs to roughly $5 trillion a year — on the order of 17 to 18 cents of every dollar of GDP, according to CMS national health expenditure accounts [S1]. As a rough, approximate split of that spending by payer: private health insurance accounts for about 3 of every 10 dollars, Medicare about 2, Medicaid about 2, households paying directly about 1, and the remainder is split between other government programs, public health activity and other third-party payers [S1]. These are national aggregates, not a quote for any individual.

Why the system looks like this

Job-based coverage grew after World War II, when wage controls made health benefits a tax-advantaged way to compete for workers; that tax exclusion still underpins most employer plans. Medicare (for older adults and some disabled people) and Medicaid (for low-income people) were created in 1965 as paired federal-state programs. The Affordable Care Act (ACA) of 2010 added the individual Marketplace, premium tax credits, Medicaid expansion (in most states) and insurance-market rules. The result is not one system but a patchwork that this book walks through.

WORKED EXAMPLE · FOLLOW $100 OF CARE

Take $100 of healthcare spending, loosely: about $30 comes from private insurance premiums and plans, about $20 from Medicare, about $20 from Medicaid, about $10 from households paying out of pocket (deductibles, copays, coinsurance and cash), and about $20 from other government and third-party sources. Every downstream chapter shows how one of those streams is organized.

Check your understanding: Is Medicare a provider of care?

No. Medicare is a payer — a federal insurance program. It writes the check; clinicians, hospitals and pharmacies provide the care. Some programs are exceptions that blur the line, such as VA health care, where the government both insures and directly delivers care through its own facilities.

  • Providers deliver, payers finance, regulators set floor rules.
  • National spending is roughly $5 trillion a year, about 17–18% of GDP.
  • Job-based coverage, Medicare, Medicaid and the ACA Marketplace are the four big coverage rails.
  • Any one person can interact with several of them in a single year.

CHAPTER 02 · SETTINGS

Where care happens

The same system meets you in very different rooms. Where you seek care determines how fast you are seen, who treats you, and what you are charged.

The main settings, from most to least acute

  • 911 and EMS: an ambulance crew (emergency medical technicians and paramedics) responds to life-threatening emergencies — chest pain, stroke signs, severe trauma — and transports to an emergency department (ED). EMS treats on scene and in transit; it is not a routine transport service.
  • Emergency room (ER): the hospital's front door for emergencies, open 24/7. Arrival triggers triage — a nurse sorts patients by urgency, so the sickest are seen first even if you arrived first. ERs can admit you to the hospital or send you home with instructions.
  • Urgent care: walk-in centers for same-day problems that are not life-threatening — sprains, minor cuts, fevers, ear infections. Typically cheaper and faster than the ER.
  • Primary care: your ongoing, first-stop clinic — a family medicine, internal medicine or pediatrics practice. It handles checkups, common illness, chronic-disease management and referrals to specialists (cardiologists, dermatologists, orthopedists and others).
  • Clinics and hospital outpatient departments: outpatient means you go home the same day. Retail clinics (in pharmacies or supermarkets) handle a narrow menu; hospital outpatient departments can bill a facility fee on top of the professional fee.
  • Prevention and screening: vaccines, cancer screenings and wellness visits — designed to catch or prevent problems early, usually the cheapest and highest-value care in the system.

Inpatient versus outpatient

Inpatient means you are formally admitted to a hospital bed, even briefly; outpatient and observation status mean you are treated and released, or monitored without formal admission. The label affects what you pay: observation stays can mean higher drug and facility bills than an admission would under the same plan, and Medicare applies different rules again.

Why setting changes the price

A clinic visit, an urgent-care visit and an ER visit for the same sore throat can differ by an order of magnitude, because each setting carries different staffing, equipment and standby costs. Plans reflect this with cost sharing: ER copays and out-of-network rates are common examples. Choosing the routine setting when it is safe to do so is the single biggest legal lever a patient has over their own bill.

Check your understanding: Should you always go to the ER for a broken bone?

No. Many fractures are evaluated safely at urgent care or a primary care clinic with an X-ray, and admitted to the ER only when surgery, uncontrolled pain or danger signs are involved. This book cannot judge your situation; if someone may be seriously hurt, call 911. The point is that the system prices settings very differently.

  • Match the setting to acuity: 911/ER for emergencies, urgent care for same-day non-emergencies, primary care for ongoing care.
  • Triage means sickest-first, not first-come-first-served.
  • Inpatient, observation and outpatient are billing statuses with real cost differences.
  • The same problem can cost an order of magnitude less in a routine setting.

CHAPTER 03 · WORKFORCE

The people who staff care

Healthcare is a team sport run in many professions, each with its own training path and legal scope. Knowing who does what makes the rest of the system legible.

The core roles

  • Physicians (MD or DO). Diagnose illness, prescribe treatment, perform procedures and surgery. Training: 4-year college, 4-year medical (or osteopathic) school, then 3–7 years of residency — longer for subspecialties. State license required; board certification voluntary.
  • Registered nurses (RNs). Assess patients, give medications, run care plans and coordinate the team. Training: associate degree (2 years) or bachelor of science in nursing (4 years), then the NCLEX-RN licensing exam. Advanced practice nurses add graduate training.
  • Nurse practitioners (NPs) and physician assistants (PAs). Diagnose, treat and prescribe, often in primary care. NPs are registered nurses with master's or doctoral training; PAs complete a roughly 2–3-year master's program, usually after a bachelor's degree. Scope and supervision rules vary by state.
  • EMTs and paramedics. The 911 backbone. EMTs complete a short certificate course (weeks to months); paramedics complete about 1–2 years and can perform advanced airway, IV and medication skills. Both work under medical oversight.
  • Pharmacists (PharmD). Dispense medicines, check for interactions and dosing, and counsel patients. Training: 4-year PharmD after prerequisite college work, plus state licensure. Pharmacy technicians support dispensing under pharmacist supervision.
  • Technicians and assistants. Phlebotomists draw blood, radiology technologists run X-ray and CT, pharmacy technicians fill prescriptions, nursing assistants help with daily care. Many roles require certificate programs and a certification exam.

Who can do what

Scope of practice — the tasks a professional is legally allowed to perform — is set by state law and can differ across state lines: some states let NPs practice independently; others require physician collaboration. Licensure belongs to a state board; employers and hospitals may grant narrower privileges on top. When two clinics give different answers, scope-of-practice law is usually why.

Check your understanding: Is a nurse practitioner a "lesser" doctor?

No — it is a different license with overlapping skills, not a rank below physicians nor a subset of nursing assistants. An NP is a registered nurse with graduate training and prescribing authority; a PA is a separate profession trained on a medical model. Physicians train longest and perform surgery; NPs and PAs deliver much of the day-to-day outpatient care in the US.

  • Physicians train longest (11–15 years); EMTs the shortest; nurses sit in the middle.
  • State boards issue licenses; certifications and hospital privileges sit on top.
  • Scope of practice varies by state — the legal answer to "who can do what" is local.
  • Team-based care is the norm, not the exception, in most settings.

CHAPTER 04 · THE COST WORDS

Health insurance basics: premiums to out-of-pocket maximums

Insurance does not make care free. It rearranges who pays, when, and how much — trading a fixed monthly price for uncertain on-the-day bills.

The six cost words

  • Premium. The fixed monthly price of coverage, paid whether or not you use care. Job-based plans usually split it with the employer, often through pre-tax payroll deduction; Marketplace buyers pay it directly or with tax-credit help.
  • Deductible. What you pay out of pocket for covered services each year before the plan starts sharing costs. $1,500 deductible means your first $1,500 of allowed costs are yours.
  • Copayment (copay). A fixed dollar amount for a service — $25 for a primary visit, $250 for the ER — usually due at the time of the visit.
  • Coinsurance. Your percentage share of the allowed amount after the deductible — 20% of $700 is $140.
  • Out-of-pocket maximum. The ceiling on your in-network cost sharing in a plan year. Hit it, and the plan pays 100% of covered in-network allowed charges for the rest of the year. Premiums do not count toward it.
  • Allowed amount. The negotiated price for a covered in-network service — the number the plan actually splits. The difference between charge and allowed amount is written off, not collected from you.

Networks: HMO, PPO, EPO and POS

  • HMO (health maintenance organization): lower premiums, care from a contracted network only (except emergencies), usually a primary care physician (PCP) gatekeeper and referrals for specialists.
  • PPO (preferred provider organization): higher premiums, broad network, see specialists without referrals; staying in-network costs less, out-of-network costs more and the plan pays less or nothing.
  • EPO (exclusive provider organization): no out-of-network coverage except emergencies, but usually no referrals; sits between HMO and PPO in price and flexibility.
  • POS (point of service): hybrid — HMO-like in-network care with PCP referrals, plus partial out-of-network coverage at higher cost to you.

In-network providers accept the plan's negotiated rate; out-of-network providers do not, and in non-emergency care you can be billed the difference — balance billing — on top of plan cost sharing. The No Surprises Act removes that risk for emergencies and for out-of-network clinicians at in-network facilities [S6]; Chapter 8 covers it in detail.

Check your understanding: Two plans — $0 deductible with high premiums, or $6,000 deductible with low premiums. Which is cheaper?

It depends on usage. In a healthy year, the high-deductible plan usually wins: low premiums and little use of care. In a bad year — surgery, a hospital stay — the low-deductible plan can win, because its out-of-pocket maximum is typically lower. Run the sliders both ways with the same care-usage number to see the crossover for yourself.

  • Premium is the monthly price of admission; cost sharing is the at-the-time split.
  • Deductible → coinsurance → out-of-pocket maximum is the exact order the plan applies them.
  • Allowed amount is the negotiated ceiling; the charge is mostly theater.
  • HMO/EPO restrict networks; PPO/POS buy flexibility with higher premiums.

CHAPTER 05 · WHO PAYS

The payers: employer plans, the Marketplace, Medicare, Medicaid, CHIP, VA and the uninsured

Every American pays through some mix of these seven doors — or through the gap between them. This chapter is the census of who pays whom.

Job with an employer — the largest single source

Employer-sponsored insurance covers the largest share of nonelderly Americans. The employer and worker usually split the premium, the worker's share often coming out of pay pre-tax. Large employers usually self-fund the plan — the company pays claims itself and hires a third-party administrator (TPA) to run the paperwork — while smaller employers more often buy a fully insured policy. COBRA lets you keep a job-based plan temporarily after leaving a job, at your own expense.

The ACA Marketplace — individual coverage

Self-employed, unemployed, or no affordable offer at work? The Marketplace is the regulated online market where individuals buy ACA-compliant plans. Premium tax credits lower the premium for most buyers based on income, and cost-sharing reductions lower deductibles for lower incomes on Silver plans. Open enrollment is once a year, with special enrollment for life events (marriage, a move, losing other coverage). See official plan categories and savings rules at HealthCare.gov [S3].

Medicare — federal insurance for older adults and some disabled people

  • Part A — hospital insurance: inpatient stays, skilled nursing facility care, hospice. Premium-free for most people who worked enough quarters.
  • Part B — medical insurance: doctor visits, outpatient care, preventive services, durable medical equipment. Monthly premium; deductible; typically 20% coinsurance after the deductible.
  • Part C — Medicare Advantage: private plans that bundle A, B and usually D, often with extra benefits and network rules.
  • Part D — prescription drugs: private plans approved by Medicare. Since 2025, out-of-pocket drug spending is capped (about $2,000 a year) for Part D enrollees.
  • Medigap — private supplemental policies that fill Part A/B cost-sharing gaps; they cannot be paired with Medicare Advantage.

Medicare is federal, administered by CMS; enrollment windows matter — missing Part B during your initial window can trigger late penalties for as long as you hold coverage. See Medicare.gov [S4].

Medicaid and CHIP — state-run coverage for low income

Medicaid is a joint federal–state program for low-income people; the federal government matches state spending, and states design and administer within federal rules, so eligibility and benefits vary by state. Most states expanded Medicaid under the ACA to most adults under 138% of the federal poverty level; a minority have not. CHIP (Children's Health Insurance Program) covers children in families that earn too much for Medicaid but not enough for private coverage [S5].

VA and TRICARE — coverage for service

The VA health system delivers care directly through its own hospitals and clinics to eligible veterans — the government is both payer and provider. TRICARE is the Department of Defense insurance program for active-duty members, their families and many retirees, using military and civilian clinicians. Veterans may hold VA coverage alongside Medicare; the two programs coordinate rather than duplicate [S10].

The uninsured and the underinsured

The uninsured — people without any coverage — still receive emergency care: federal screening and stabilization requirements mean Medicare-participating hospitals must stabilize emergency patients regardless of ability to pay. They are then billed the full charge, though financial assistance ("charity care") and the No Surprises Act good-faith-estimate rights may reduce the bill. The underinsured — covered on paper with deductibles they cannot pay — are increasingly common; their protection is the out-of-pocket maximum, which exists precisely for the catastrophic year.

Check your understanding: Does Medicare cover long-term nursing home care?

Generally no. Medicare covers short-term skilled nursing care after a qualifying hospital stay, not custodial long-term care. Medicaid is the main public payer for long-term custodial care, subject to strict income and asset rules — which is why long-term care planning is its own discipline.

  • Job-based coverage is the biggest door; the employer usually splits the premium.
  • Medicare: Part A hospital, Part B medical, Part C Medicare Advantage, Part D drugs.
  • Medicaid is state-run within federal rules; CHIP covers children in moderate-income families.
  • The Marketplace sells ACA-compliant individual plans with income-based subsidies.
  • Uninsured people still get emergency stabilization — then the bill.

CHAPTER 06 · ONE VISIT

A doctor visit, from scheduling to the bill

A routine visit is a short, predictable machine: schedule, check in, examine, document, code, claim, EOB, bill. Knowing the gears saves real money and real anxiety.

Before you walk in

  • Scheduling. The front desk verifies the appointment, your demographics and — ideally — insurance eligibility. Errors here (wrong ID number, lapsed coverage) are a top cause of denied claims later.
  • Referrals. A referral is your PCP's written order to see a specialist; HMOs and many POS plans require one, PPOs and EPOs generally do not. No referral, no coverage, in plans that require it.
  • Prior authorization. The plan's advance approval that a service, drug or procedure is medically necessary and covered — typically for expensive imaging, specialty drugs, elective procedures and some therapies. It is a coverage gate, not a clinical opinion; it can be denied on paperwork grounds alone. Emergency care never requires one in advance.
  • Check-in. Bring your insurance card and photo ID, expect a demographic review and the collection of a copay if one applies.

The visit itself

The clinician takes a history, examines you, orders tests or treatment, and documents all of it in the medical record. Documentation is the raw material for billing: if it is not written down, it did not happen as far as the claim is concerned.

After you leave

  • Coding. A coder translates the note into ICD-10-CM diagnosis codes and CPT procedure codes (Chapter 7).
  • Claim and adjudication. The claim goes to the insurer, usually via a clearinghouse; the insurer prices it at the negotiated allowed amount and applies your cost sharing.
  • Paper trail. Within weeks you receive an Explanation of Benefits (EOB) — not a bill — followed by the provider's bill for your share, which should match the EOB line for line.

PRACTICAL CHECKLIST

Before an appointment: confirm the office takes your in-network status, ask whether prior authorization is required, confirm the copay, bring your card and medication list, and ask for the visit's expected codes if the care is expensive. Afterward: match the EOB against the bill, and file anything that disagrees before paying.

Check your understanding: The ER says you need a non-emergency MRI in two weeks. What should you check first?

Whether your plan requires prior authorization for that MRI and whether the imaging center is in-network. An out-of-network center or a missing prior authorization can turn a covered service into a full-price one — the single most expensive click in American healthcare is the one made before the appointment.

  • Referrals route you between clinicians; prior authorization gates coverage for specific care.
  • Emergency care never waits for advance approval.
  • Documentation becomes codes; codes become claims; claims become EOBs; EOBs precede bills.
  • The cheapest fix for billing errors is at scheduling, not after the EOB arrives.

CHAPTER 07 · PAPER TRAIL

Billing and claims: codes, rates and the EOB decoded

A healthcare bill is the visible tip of a claims machine. Once you can read the machine — codes, allowed amounts, adjudication, the EOB — the paperwork stops being intimidating.

The two code systems

  • ICD-10-CM (diagnosis codes). Why you were seen — S52.501A, for instance, is an unspecified fracture of the lower end of the radius, initial encounter. Thousands of codes; the diagnosis is the medical justification for everything billed after it.
  • CPT (procedure codes). What was done — 73100 is a wrist X-ray, single view; 99213 is a mid-complexity office visit. CPT is maintained by the American Medical Association; related HCPCS codes cover supplies, ambulance rides and drugs.
  • Codes are paired: diagnosis justifies procedure. A wrist X-ray billed under a back-pain diagnosis will be refused — not because the X-ray was wrong, but because the pairing failed a coverage edit.

Chargemaster versus the allowed amount

Hospitals keep a chargemaster — the list of sticker prices for every service, historically used as a starting point for negotiation and still the baseline for the uninsured. Almost nobody pays it. In-network providers have contracts: the plan's allowed amount is the real price, and the gap — in the worked example, $4,530.00 of the $9,500.00 chargemaster total — is written off and cannot be balance-billed to an in-network patient.

HOW TO READ ANY EOB IN 30 SECONDS

1) Find the allowed amount — that is the real price. 2) See how much went to your deductible. 3) Check the coinsurance math on what remains. 4) Confirm the plan's payment plus your share equals the allowed amount. 5) Compare the EOB with the provider's bill — they must agree. Any mismatch is worth a phone call to the plan and the provider, in that order.

The clearinghouse and the five gates

Most claims pass through a clearinghouse — a switch that validates file format, fixes obvious errors and routes the claim to the right payer. The insurer then runs its adjudication gates in order: (1) is the patient eligible and is the coverage active; (2) is the service a covered benefit; (3) is the provider in-network; (4) is the service medically necessary for the ICD-10 diagnosis billed; (5) what cost sharing applies after the deductible and coinsurance? Fail any gate and the claim pays zero — or less than expected.

Check your understanding: The EOB shows "allowed amount: $0.00." What happened?

The claim was denied or not covered — often a coding mismatch, a missing prior authorization, a non-covered benefit or an eligibility problem. "Allowed $0.00" is the plan's way of saying the claim failed a gate. Read the denial reason code, then see Chapter 8.

  • ICD-10 says why; CPT says what. The pairing must make clinical sense.
  • The chargemaster is a sticker price; the allowed amount is the contract price.
  • An EOB is not a bill. It is the receipt for the claim split.
  • EOB plus provider bill must agree — check them against each other, line by line.

CHAPTER 08 · WHEN THINGS GO WRONG

Denials and appeals — and the No Surprises Act

A denial is a negotiating position, not a verdict. Plans deny claims routinely and reverse many on appeal; the system even has an independent referee. Here is how to use it.

Why claims get denied

  • Coding mismatch. Diagnosis and procedure codes do not pair logically, or a code is missing digits. Fixed by resubmission.
  • Missing prior authorization. The service needed advance approval that was never obtained (or was obtained for the wrong code). Often appealable if care was urgent.
  • Not medically necessary. The plan's medical policy does not accept the treatment as necessary for the diagnosis. The classic internal-appeal target, with physician letters.
  • Not a covered benefit. The plan excludes the service outright — cosmetic care, experimental therapy, routine dental for adults. Usually final, barring narrow exceptions.
  • Eligibility / coverage lapsed. The plan shows you were not covered on the date of service — frequently an employer data error; fixable with proof.
  • Duplicate or unbundled claim. Billed twice, or components that must be billed together were split. Chapter 11's ER physician claim started here.
  • Out-of-network with no authorization. In non-emergency, non-protected care you may owe more. This is where the No Surprises Act changes the rules.

Your appeal rights

  • Read the denial letter first. It must state the specific reason and your right to appeal. Deadlines are real: group plans (employer coverage) generally give at least 180 days from the denial to file an internal appeal; Marketplace plans set their own windows shown in the letter [S8].
  • Internal appeal. Ask the plan to reconsider, in writing. Attach your own summary, the denial letter, relevant records and a letter of medical necessity from your clinician. Keep a copy of everything and note dates.
  • Standard turnaround is usually 30 days for pre-service denials of group plans and 60 days for post-service; urgent-care denials must be decided within 72 hours.
  • External review. If the internal appeal fails, an independent review organization decides. Its decision is binding on the plan — meaning if you win, the plan must pay.
  • Regulators. Job-based plans: the Department of Labor's Employee Benefits Security Administration helps workers understand and enforce their rights [S8]. Individual-market plans: your state insurance department. Medicare: 1-800-MEDICARE / the IRE process.

Surprise billing and the No Surprises Act

A surprise bill is a balance bill from an out-of-network clinician you could not choose — the anesthesiologist you never met, the ER physician group that took you in. Since January 1, 2022 the No Surprises Act bars these in three situations [S6, S7]:

  • Emergency services, wherever you receive them, without prior authorization and at in-network cost sharing.
  • Non-emergency care at an in-network facility delivered by out-of-network clinicians (radiology, anesthesia, pathology, assistant surgeons, and similar), unless you give advance written consent to waive the protection — which is rarely allowed for those ancillary services at all.
  • Air ambulance services delivered out-of-network.

If you are uninsured or pay cash, providers must give a good faith estimate of expected charges on request or three business days before scheduled care; if the final bill exceeds the estimate by $400 or more, you can dispute it through a patient-provider dispute resolution process [S6]. Ground ambulance rides are, as of this writing, not covered by the federal No Surprises Act; some states fill that gap — one of the law's known open seams. Complaints go to the No Surprises Help Desk at 1-800-985-3059 [S6].

WORKED EXAMPLE · THE ER PHYSICIAN DENIAL

Chapter 11's episode: the out-of-network ER physician's claim was denied as "duplicate of facility claim — possible coding error." The provider corrected the claim to reference the facility claim separately and resubmitted; when the corrected claim was again refused, Maya's clinic filed an internal appeal with the operative note attached. The plan reversed the denial and paid $560.00; Maya's share stayed $140.00. Nothing about the care changed — only the paperwork.

Check your understanding: You get a $3,200 bill from an out-of-network radiologist two months after an in-network colonoscopy. What are your rights?

The No Surprises Act should cap your share at in-network cost sharing, because radiology at an in-network facility is a protected ancillary service with no valid consent mechanism to waive protection. Pay only the in-network amount shown on your EOB, send the provider the EOB, and call the No Surprises Help Desk (1-800-985-3059) if they persist. If the facility or insurer believes an exception genuinely applies, they must prove it — the burden is on them, not you.

  • Denial ≠ final. Resubmit, appeal internally, then go external.
  • External review decisions bind the plan.
  • Keep every letter; deadlines run from the date on the denial.
  • The No Surprises Act covers emergencies, in-network-facility ancillary care and air ambulance — not ground ambulance.

CHAPTER 09 · DRUGS

Prescription drugs: pharmacies, PBMs, formularies and why prices vary

Every pill has two prices — the one on the bottle and the one behind it. Understanding the pharmacy benefit chain explains almost every invoice you have ever stared at.

Brand, generic and biosimilar

  • Brand-name drugs are the innovator's patented product — usually expensive, usually tiered high on a formulary.
  • Generic drugs contain the same active ingredient at the same dose and are approved by the FDA as therapeutically equivalent — same effect, often a small fraction of the price. Switching from brand to generic is the highest-confidence way to cut a drug bill.
  • Biosimilars are the biologic equivalent: highly similar, not identical, cheaper copies of complex protein drugs.

The PBM's job — and the controversy

A pharmacy benefit manager administers drug benefits for health plans and employers: it builds the formulary (the covered-drug list), sets tier cost sharing, negotiates rebates from manufacturers and pays pharmacies. In exchange, manufacturers that win preferred tiers pay PBMs rebates — typically a percentage of list price. The open questions, debated by Congress, regulators and courts: rebates can reward higher list prices over cheaper alternatives; spread pricing lets a PBM charge the plan more than it pays the pharmacy and keep the difference; and patients at the counter usually pay coinsurance on the list price, not the rebated net price. Recent transparency rules and the No Surprises Act-adjacent oversight aim at exactly these gaps [S8].

Why the same drug costs different amounts

  1. List vs net. The list price is public; the net price after rebates is negotiated and private.
  2. Tier placement. Preferred generic (often $0–$10), generic, brand, preferred brand, specialty — each tier has its own copay or coinsurance.
  3. Formulary design. Plans exclude drugs and require prior authorization or step therapy (try the cheap one first) for others.
  4. Channel. Retail vs mail order vs specialty pharmacy; manufacturer coupons for commercially insured patients (unusable by Medicare/Medicaid enrollees).
  5. Site of care. Many infused drugs are cheaper in a standalone clinic than in a hospital outpatient department, because the billing codes and facility fees differ.

Numbers a beginner should recognize

Three anchors: (1) Since 2025, Medicare Part D out-of-pocket drug spending is capped at about $2,000 a year, and Medicare-covered insulin is capped at $35 per month. (2) ACA-compliant plans cover recommended preventive vaccines at $0 cost sharing. (3) A widely available $4-style generic discount exists for hundreds of chronic-medication fills — a cash price can be lower than the plan copay at expensive tiers; comparing both is always worthwhile.

Check your understanding: A brand drug costs $180/month on your plan; the generic is $8. What is the catch?

Usually there is none — generics are FDA-verified as therapeutically equivalent. The exceptions involve a narrow-therapeutic-index drug where the prescriber deliberately wants one manufacturer's product, or a prior-authorization wrinkle. Ask the prescriber whether substitution is appropriate; if yes, it saves more than any other single action on the list above.

  • The PBM — not the shelf — sets what most insured people pay for drugs.
  • Generics and biosimilars are the biggest structural savings in drug spending.
  • List price is not net price; rebates mostly benefit plans and PBMs.
  • Ask about tier, formulary status, prior auth and mail-order alternatives for any expensive prescription.

CHAPTER 10 · THE OTHER HALF

Public health and population health

Most of the health you have was decided before you felt anything. Public health works on communities instead of individuals — and it is the cheapest medicine ever invented.

Who does what

  • Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). The national public health agency: disease surveillance through networks like NREVSS and PulseNet, immunization schedules, the Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report (MMWR), travel health and laboratory reference services [S11].
  • State health departments. License facilities and clinicians, run state immunization registries, investigate outbreaks, manage vital statistics (births, deaths), and administer Medicaid alongside CMS, and WIC and vital programs with USDA and CDC.
  • Local and tribal health departments. Restaurant and water inspection, lead abatement, contact tracing, community vaccine clinics, needle exchanges, maternal-child programs — the visible, boots-on-the-ground layer.
  • Other federal players. FDA approves drugs and devices; NIH funds research; CMS pays for the care itself and sets hospital quality and emergency standards; the US Public Health Service Commissioned Corps staffs many of these agencies.

Vaccination and screening: what "population health" buys

Vaccines are the clearest case: each immunized person absorbs a small cost and risk; the community returns herd immunity, which protects those who cannot be vaccinated. Screening — cancer, blood pressure, cholesterol, newborn screens — finds disease before symptoms, when treatment is cheaper and gentler. Both are population-level bets made one arm at a time [S9].

How it differs from personal medical care

  • Unit of care. One patient versus a population or place (a county, a school, a watershed).
  • Goal. Cure or manage disease versus prevent it and shorten its spread.
  • Payment. Bills and claims versus taxes, grants and categorical funding (braiding, not billing).
  • Evidence. Randomized trials and clinical guidelines versus surveillance data, outbreak investigation and cost-effectiveness analysis.
  • Failure mode. A missed diagnosis versus a missed signal — an outbreak caught two weeks late.

WORKED EXAMPLE · THE FRACTURE AS A PUBLIC-HEALTH DATA POINT

Maya's wrist fracture looks like one bill — but every emergency department reports syndromic data to public health systems. If dozens of identical ice-related fractures hit the same week, local health departments can see it in surveillance before the news does, and act: warning signage, sidewalk treatment, school closures. Personal care fixes Maya's arm; population health prevents the next hundred wrists.

Check your understanding: Your neighbor has measles symptoms. Is your pediatrician paid to handle this, or is someone else involved?

Both. Your pediatrician treats your neighbor (personal care); the local health department is notified the same day — measles is a reportable, vaccine-preventable disease — and it investigates contacts, verifies vaccination status and organizes containment (population health). Reportable diseases are one of the few times the two halves work from the same clipboard.

  • Public health serves communities; personal care serves patients — same system, different unit of work.
  • CDC surveils and guides; states license and coordinate; local departments act.
  • Vaccines and screening are population health delivered one person at a time.
  • Surveillance is how the system sees the wave before it arrives.

The denial and appeal, exactly

The ER physician's $700.00-allowed claim was denied as an apparent duplicate of the facility claim. The provider corrected and resubmitted it; when it was refused again, Maya's clinic filed an internal appeal within the 180-day window with the operative note attached. The plan reversed the denial and paid $560.00; her share stayed $140.00 — the same amount she would have owed had the claim paid cleanly the first time. Appeals change who pays whom, not the price of the care. The No Surprises Act independently barred the ER physician group from balance-billing her the other $400.00 it charged beyond the allowed amount.

What changes with a different plan

  • Uninsured. Maya would face the $9,500.00 chargemaster amount, though she could ask for a good faith estimate and financial assistance, and dispute bills at least $400.00 above the estimate [S6].
  • High-deductible plan. With a $3,000.00 deductible unmet, she would owe every allowed dollar until the deductible was satisfied — $4,970.00 in cost sharing, and the insurer would pay $0.00 this episode.
  • Out-of-pocket maximum reached earlier in the year. She would owe nothing further this plan year; the plan pays 100% of allowed in-network charges.
  • No Surprises Act edge cases. The protection covers the ER physician and air ambulance — but ground ambulance balance billing can still happen in many states; always ask whether an ambulance ride is in-network when it is not an emergency [S6].
Final check: Maya's friend says "the hospital made $4,530.00 in free discounts — so the system isn't really expensive." What is the right frame?

The write-off is the contract, not generosity: the hospital already agreed that $4,970.00 settles the claim in full for in-network patients. The real costs of the system live in the total — premiums ($5,760.00 for the default plan in Chapter 4's simulator), cost sharing ($1,794.00 here), employer contributions and taxes (Medicare and Medicaid). Individual bills are the visible edge of a $5-trillion machine.

  • Allowed − plan − you = 0, always. If your paperwork disagrees, something is miscoded.
  • One episode produced five claims from five billing entities — normal for a single ER visit.
  • The denial changed nothing about the care or the price; only who wrote the check, and only after appeal.
  • Compare every bill to the EOB; the EOB wins unless corrected.

Glossary

Short, plain-language definitions. Plan contracts, state law and clinical circumstance can all change the exact meaning; the chapter links show where each term is used.

Payer
Any party that pays for health care: an insurer, an employer self-funding a plan, or a government program. Providers deliver care; payers finance it.
CMS
The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services, the federal agency that runs Medicare, Medicaid's federal side and the federal Marketplace, and writes many payment and quality rules.
Affordable Care Act (ACA)
The 2010 health reform law that created the Marketplaces, premium tax credits and insurance-market rules, and expanded Medicaid in most states.
Marketplace (exchange)
The regulated online market where individuals buy ACA-compliant plans, with income-based subsidies; HealthCare.gov or a state-run site.
Emergency medical services (EMS)
911-dispatched ambulance crews — EMTs and paramedics — who stabilize and transport patients in emergencies.
Emergency department (ED / ER)
The hospital's 24/7 front door for emergencies. Triage sorts by urgency; the sickest go first.
Triage
Sorting patients by clinical urgency so the most unstable are treated first, not the first to arrive.
Urgent care
Walk-in same-day care for problems that are not life- or limb-threatening but should not wait for a scheduled visit.
Primary care
Ongoing, first-contact care: checkups, common illness, chronic-disease management and referrals. Delivered by family medicine, internal medicine and pediatrics.
Specialist
A clinician focused on an organ system or service — cardiology, orthopedics, dermatology. Seeing one may require a referral in HMO and POS plans.
Clinic
Any ambulatory care site: physician practices, retail clinics, community health centers, hospital outpatient departments.
Inpatient
Formally admitted to a hospital bed. Admission status changes billing, drug charges and sometimes your out-of-pocket maximum.
Outpatient
Treated and released the same day — clinic visits, most surgery, most imaging.
Observation status
Monitored in a hospital bed without formal admission. Bills like outpatient care, sometimes with higher drug and facility costs.
Physician (MD / DO)
A doctor licensed by a state medical board after college, medical/osteopathic school and residency. DOs train with additional emphasis on whole-person, osteopathic manipulative technique.
Registered nurse (RN)
Assesses patients, administers medications, runs care plans and coordinates the team. Licensed by state boards of nursing after approved training and the NCLEX-RN.
NCLEX-RN
The standardized licensing exam every US registered nurse must pass. There is a separate NCLEX-PN for practical nurses.
Nurse practitioner (NP)
An RN with graduate training who diagnoses, treats and prescribes, often in primary care. Independent practice authority varies by state.
Physician assistant (PA)
A clinician trained on a medical model in a roughly two-to-three-year master's program; diagnoses, treats and prescribes under state-defined supervision or collaboration.
EMT (emergency medical technician)
Front-line 911 clinician: airway, CPR, bleeding control, splints, medications limited to a short approved list. Certificate training, months.
Paramedic
Advanced 911 clinician: IVs, advanced airways, cardiac monitoring and a wider drug formulary. Roughly one to two years of training after EMT.
Pharmacist
Doctor of pharmacy (PharmD): dispenses medicines, verifies interactions and dosing, and counsels patients. State license required.
Technician / assistant
Support roles: phlebotomists (blood draws), radiology technologists (imaging), pharmacy and laboratory technicians, nursing assistants. Often certificate programs plus a certification exam.
Scope of practice
The tasks a clinician is legally allowed to perform, set by state law. It changes — see your state's licensing board.
Credentialing and privileges
Beyond a state license, hospitals verify training and grant "privileges" — permission to perform specific procedures at that facility.
Premium
The fixed monthly price of coverage, paid whether or not you use care. In job-based plans, usually split with the employer, pre-tax.
Deductible
What you pay for covered services each plan year before the plan starts sharing costs. Resets annually.
Copayment (copay)
A fixed dollar amount for a service, usually at the time of care — e.g., $25 primary visit, $250 ER.
Coinsurance
Your percentage share of the allowed amount after the deductible — 20% of $700.00 is $140.00.
Out-of-pocket maximum
The annual ceiling on your in-network deductible, copays and coinsurance. Hit it and the plan pays 100% of covered in-network allowed costs; premiums never count.
Allowed amount
The negotiated price for a covered in-network service — the number the plan splits with you. Charges above it are written off, not billed to you.
HMO
Health maintenance organization: contracted network only (emergencies excepted), typically a primary-care gatekeeper and referral requirement; lower premiums.
PPO
Preferred provider organization: broad network, no referrals; lower cost in-network, higher cost or no coverage out-of-network; higher premiums.
EPO
Exclusive provider organization: in-network only (emergencies excepted), usually no referrals; priced between HMO and PPO.
POS
Point of service: HMO-like care with a primary-care gatekeeper in-network, plus partial out-of-network coverage at higher cost.
Primary care physician (PCP)
Your ongoing clinician and, in HMO and POS plans, the referral gatekeeper.
Referral
Your PCP's written order for specialist care. Required in HMO and many POS plans; not required in PPO/EPO.
In-network
Providers who signed the plan's rate contract. You pay only cost sharing; balance billing is not allowed.
Out-of-network
Providers without a rate contract. Non-emergency OON care can mean full charges plus balance billing; emergencies are protected by the No Surprises Act.
Balance billing
Billing the patient the difference between the provider's charge and the plan's allowed amount — banned for in-network and for No Surprises Act-protected care.
Actuarial value
Rough percentage of average allowed costs a plan pays — Bronze ≈60%, Silver ≈70%, Gold ≈80%, Platinum ≈90%. Not a quality rating.
Employer-sponsored insurance (ESI)
Health coverage offered through a job, usually with the employer sharing the premium; the largest single source of US coverage.
Self-funded plan
The employer pays claims itself rather than buying insurance; usually administered by a third-party administrator. Federal ERISA law governs most.
Third-party administrator (TPA)
The company that runs a self-funded plan's paperwork: claims, networks, ID cards — without bearing the insurance risk.
Fully insured plan
The employer buys an insurance policy; the insurer bears the claim risk and is state-regulated.
COBRA
Federal right to keep employer coverage temporarily after job loss or other qualifying events — usually at your full cost plus 2%.
Premium tax credit
Income-based tax credit that lowers Marketplace premiums; paid monthly in advance or claimed at filing.
Cost-sharing reduction (CSR)
Extra help lowering deductibles and copays on Silver Marketplace plans for lower incomes.
Medicare Advantage (Part C)
Private plans bundling Parts A, B (usually D), often with extra benefits and network rules, paid a capitated rate per enrollee.
Medigap
Standardized private supplements filling Original Medicare cost-sharing gaps. Not combinable with Medicare Advantage.
Medicaid
Joint federal–state insurance for low-income people. States administer within federal rules; eligibility and benefits vary by state.
CHIP
Children's Health Insurance Program: coverage for children in families above Medicaid income limits but below private affordability.
TRICARE
Department of Defense health program for active-duty service members, their families and many retirees.
VA health care
Veterans' care delivered directly by the Department of Veterans Affairs through its own hospitals and clinics.
Uninsured
No coverage of any kind. Emergency stabilization is required regardless of payment ability; the bill still comes.
Underinsured
Covered on paper, but deductibles and out-of-pocket maximums exceed what the household can absorb.
Financial assistance (charity care)
Hospital and plan programs that reduce or forgive bills for low-income patients; nonprofit hospitals are required to maintain such policies.
Prior authorization (prior auth)
Advance plan approval that a service, drug or device is medically necessary and covered. A coverage gate, not a clinical judgment; urgent and emergency care is exempt in practice.
Medical record
The clinical documentation of your care — the only thing your chart knows and the raw material every bill is built from.
ICD-10-CM
The diagnosis code system: why you were seen. S52.501A is an unspecified fracture of the lower radius, initial encounter.
CPT
Current Procedural Terminology — the procedure code system: what was done. 73100 is a wrist X-ray, single view.
HCPCS
Codes for things CPT doesn't cover: supplies, ambulance transport, drugs and DME, in Level II (A-codes, J-codes and friends).
Clearinghouse
A switch between providers and payers that validates file format, fixes routine errors and routes claims.
Explanation of Benefits (EOB)
The insurer's itemized statement of allowed amount, deductible application, plan payment and your share. Not a bill.
Write-off (adjustment)
The difference between charge and allowed amount that a contracted provider gives up and may not bill to an in-network patient.
Chargemaster
The hospital's internal list of sticker prices. Rarely the real price; anchors the uninsured bill before discounts.
External review
Independent re-adjudication by an outside organization after an internal appeal fails. Binding on the plan if it upholds you.
Surprise bill
A balance bill from an out-of-network clinician you had no meaningful ability to choose or avoid — now restricted by the No Surprises Act.
No Surprises Act
2022 federal law: emergency OON care, OON clinicians at in-network facilities, and air ambulances must be treated as in-network for cost sharing and cannot balance-bill you.
Good faith estimate
For uninsured or self-pay patients, a provider's written estimate of expected charges given on request or before scheduled care; bills ≥$400 above it are disputable.
Pharmacy benefit manager (PBM)
The administrator of the drug benefit: formulary design, rebate negotiation, pharmacy reimbursement — for plans and employers.
Formulary
The list of drugs a plan covers, with tiers and any prior-authorization or step-therapy rules attached.
Drug tier
Cost-sharing rung in the formulary — preferred generic, generic, brand, preferred brand, specialty — each with its own copay or coinsurance.
Rebate
Money flowing from manufacturer to PBM (often shared with the plan) in exchange for formulary placement — a list-price discount invisible at the counter.
Spread pricing
A PBM practice of charging the plan more than it reimburses the pharmacy and keeping the difference; under regulatory fire.
Brand-name drug
The innovator's patented product. Expensive; tiered high; usually the first to be authorized as generic after patent expiry.
Generic drug
FDA-approved therapeutic equivalent of a brand drug: same active ingredient, strength and route. Far cheaper.
Biosimilar
A highly similar, non-identical copy of a biologic drug; approved as safe and effective, priced below the reference product.
CDC
Centers for Disease Control and Prevention: the national public health agency — surveillance, immunization policy, outbreak response, MMWR.
MMWR
Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report — the CDC's weekly bulletin of public health findings and outbreak reports.
Vaccine
A preparation that trains the immune system against a disease; the foundation of population-level protection.
Screening
Testing before symptoms exist — cancer, blood pressure, cholesterol, newborns — to catch disease when it is cheapest to treat.
