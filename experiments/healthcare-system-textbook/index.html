CHAPTER 01 · FOUNDATION

The big picture: who provides, who pays, who regulates

Three questions unlock the system: who provides care, who pays, who writes the rules. Every visit in this book travels between those three groups.

The three jobs

  • Providers deliver care: hospitals, physician practices, urgent care, emergency medical services (EMS), pharmacies, labs, imaging.
  • Payers finance care: insurers and government programs collect premiums or taxes and pay negotiated rates.
  • Regulators set floor rules: federal agencies govern Medicare, Medicaid and the marketplace; states license clinicians, hospitals and insurers; CMS runs Medicare, the federal marketplace and fraud and quality rules.

Where the money comes from

U.S. health spending reached $5.3 trillion in 2024 ($15,474/person) — 18.0% of GDP, projected past 20% by 2034 [S1, S12]. Split: private insurance 31%, Medicare 21%, Medicaid 18%, out-of-pocket 11%, other payers 19% [S12]. Hospitals take 31%, and market concentration is steep: by 2024, 69% of hospitals belonged to a health system (up from 56% in 2010), 97% of metropolitan areas were highly concentrated for inpatient care, and one or two systems controlled 100% of inpatient care in 47% of metro areas [S12, S34]. After ~1,200 mergers over two decades—including cross-market acquisitions of hospitals in other metros (~17% higher prices than stand-alone peers)—consolidation raises commercial prices without quality gains [S13, S34].

Why the system looks like this

Job-based coverage grew after WWII, when wage controls made tax-advantaged benefits a hiring tool; that tax exclusion still underpins it. The Affordable Care Act (ACA) of 2010 added the Marketplace, premium tax credits, Medicaid expansion and market rules — a patchwork this book unpacks.

WORKED EXAMPLE · FOLLOW $100 OF CARE

Take $100 of spending: ~$31 private insurance, ~$21 Medicare, ~$18 Medicaid, ~$11 out of pocket (deductibles, copays, coinsurance, cash), ~$19 other government and third-party payers [S12].

Check your understanding: Is Medicare a provider of care?

No. Medicare is a payer — a federal insurance program. It writes the check; clinicians, hospitals and pharmacies provide care. The blurred exception is VA health care, where government insures and delivers through its own facilities.

CHAPTER 02 · SETTINGS

Where care happens

Where you seek care decides how fast you are seen, who treats you, and what you are charged.

The main settings, from most to least acute

  • 911 and EMS: crews treat on scene and in transit, then transport to an emergency department (ED). Not routine transport.
  • Emergency room (ER): the 24/7 front door. Triage on arrival — sickest first. Outcome: admission or discharge.
  • Urgent care: walk-ins for same-day non-emergencies — sprains, minor cuts, fevers. Cheaper and faster than the ER.
  • Primary care: your ongoing, first-stop clinic — family medicine, internal medicine, pediatrics. Checkups, common illness, chronic disease, specialist referrals.
  • Clinics and hospital outpatient departments: same-day, go-home care. Retail clinics run a narrow menu; hospital outpatient departments may add a facility fee.
  • Prevention and screening: vaccines, cancer screenings, wellness visits — the cheapest, highest-value care there is.

Inpatient versus outpatient

Inpatient: formally admitted, even briefly. Outpatient and observation: treated and released, or monitored without admission. The label changes your bill — observation can cost more than an admission, with different Medicare rules again.

Why setting changes the price

An ER sore throat can cost ten times a clinic visit — standby staffing and equipment. Ownership matters too: hospital-bought clinics can triple prices via outpatient facility fees (e.g., $200 vs. $600 knee injection), fueling site-neutral payment reform (Penn LDI) [S13]. Telehealth (remote video or phone care) episodes averaged $96 vs. $509 in-person with fewer follow-ups across 160,000+ visits, though mental-health charges were similar (Penn Medicine, 2026) [S14]. Match setting to urgency to cut your bill.

Place-of-service codes, facility fees and practice ownership

Claims tell payers where care happened using two-digit Place of Service (POS) codes [S35]. An independent doctor's office bills POS 11 (one bundled payment under Medicare's Physician Fee Schedule covering both physician work and office overhead) [S35, S36]. When a hospital owns that clinic as a hospital outpatient department (HOPD)—POS 22 on campus or POS 19 off campus—the visit splits into two claims: a reduced clinician "facility rate" plus a separate hospital facility fee under outpatient hospital rates [S35, S36].

Because the combined HOPD payout routinely exceeds the freestanding office rate, hospitals gain revenue by buying practices and reclassifying them. Medicare's partial site-neutral rules for newer off-campus clinics saved $1.2 billion in 2024, and CMS's 2026 outpatient rule cut off-campus HOPD drug-administration rates by 60% (projected $8 billion savings over ten years) [S37, S38]. On-campus HOPDs remain exempt; CBO estimates aligning all routine office-equivalent HOPD rates would save $157 billion over a decade [S38].

Corporate consolidation changes prices even without facility fees. Small independent practices face steep billing overhead and weak insurer leverage, driving vertical integration (by 2023, 72% of cardiologists and 57% of gastroenterologists were hospital-employed) and private equity (PE) buyouts [S39]. Across national commercial data, hospital-affiliated practices negotiated 10.7% higher primary-care prices and 16.3%–20.7% higher cardiology and GI prices than independents; PE-backed practices charged 20.2% (+$71/claim) more, raised allowed amounts 11.0% (+$23/claim), grew volume 16.3%, and won 6.0%–10.0% higher commercial specialty prices—often via small roll-ups below federal merger-reporting thresholds [S34, S39, S40, S41].

Check your understanding: Should you always go to the ER for a broken bone?

No. Many fractures are evaluated safely at urgent care or primary care with an X-ray; ER care is for surgery, uncontrolled pain or danger signs. Seriously hurt? Call 911. The system prices settings very differently.

CHAPTER 03 · WORKFORCE

The people who staff care

Many professions, each with its own training path and legal scope. Knowing who does what makes the rest legible.

The core roles

  • Physicians (MD or DO). Diagnose, prescribe, perform procedures and surgery. Path: 4-year college, 4-year medical school, 3–7 years of residency — longer for subspecialties. State license; board certification voluntary.
  • Registered nurses (RNs). Assess, medicate, run care plans, coordinate the team. Path: associate or bachelor's in nursing, then the NCLEX-RN exam. Advanced practice nurses add graduate training.
  • Nurse practitioners (NPs) and physician assistants (PAs). Diagnose, treat and prescribe, often in primary care. NPs are registered nurses with master's or doctoral training; PAs complete a 2–3-year master's program after a bachelor's. Scope and supervision rules vary by state.
  • EMTs and paramedics. The 911 backbone. EMTs: a short certificate course (weeks to months). Paramedics: about 1–2 years, advanced airway, IV and medication skills. Both work under medical oversight.
  • Pharmacists (PharmD). Dispense medicines, check interactions and dosing, counsel patients. Training: 4-year PharmD after college, plus state licensure. Technicians support dispensing under pharmacist supervision.
  • Technicians and assistants. Phlebotomists draw blood, radiology techs run X-ray and CT, pharmacy techs fill prescriptions, nursing assistants help with daily care. Many roles: certificate programs plus an exam.

Who can do what

Scope of practice is state law: some states let NPs practice independently; others require physician collaboration. Hospitals add narrower privileges on top. Fewer patients per hospital nurse also lowers 30-day deaths, infections, stays and turnover — linking "shortages" to workload (Penn Nursing / LDI) [S15].

NPIs, taxonomy codes and payer enrollment

State licenses let a clinician practice, but insurers cannot pay a claim without a federal National Provider Identifier (NPI)—a 10-digit, intelligence-free ID issued through CMS's NPPES system [S42]. An NPI has two types: Type 1 (individual) stays with one clinician for life (including unincorporated sole proprietors), while Type 2 (organization) identifies hospitals, clinics, group practices, or incorporated solo practices tied to an employer tax ID [S42].

Every NPI links to one or more 10-character taxonomy codes maintained by the National Uniform Claim Committee—for example, 207Q00000X for Family Medicine—specifying specialty and classification [S43]. Having an NPI does not verify licensure or network status; instead, clinicians submit their Type 1 NPI, group Type 2 NPI, and taxonomy codes during hospital credentialing, Medicare PECOS enrollment, and insurer contracting [S42]. Every claim reports both numbers: Type 2 for who gets paid, Type 1 for who delivered the care [S42, S43].

Check your understanding: Is a nurse practitioner a "lesser" doctor?

No — a different license with overlapping skills, not a rank below physicians. An NP is a registered nurse with graduate training and prescribing authority; a PA is a separate profession trained on a medical model. Both deliver much day-to-day outpatient care.

CHAPTER 04 · THE COST WORDS

Health insurance basics: premiums to out-of-pocket maximums

Insurance does not make care free. It rearranges who pays, when, how much — a fixed monthly price for uncertain at-the-visit bills.

The six cost words

  • Premium. Fixed monthly price of coverage, paid whether or not you use care. Job plans usually split it, often pre-tax; Marketplace buyers pay directly or with tax-credit help.
  • Deductible. What you pay for covered services each year before the plan shares costs. $1,500 means your first $1,500 of allowed costs are yours.
  • Copayment (copay). Fixed dollar amount per service — $25 primary, $250 ER — usually due at the visit.
  • Coinsurance. Your percentage share of the allowed amount after the deductible — 20% of $700 is $140.
  • Out-of-pocket maximum. The ceiling on in-network cost sharing in a plan year. Hit it; the plan pays 100% of covered in-network allowed charges; premiums never count.
  • Allowed amount. The negotiated price for covered in-network services — the number the plan actually splits. Charge minus allowed is written off, never collected.

Networks: HMO, PPO, EPO and POS

  • HMO (health maintenance organization): network only (emergencies excepted); usually a primary care physician (PCP) gatekeeper and referrals; lower premiums.
  • PPO (preferred provider organization): higher premiums, broad network, no referrals; in-network cheaper, out-of-network costlier or uncovered.
  • EPO (exclusive provider organization): in-network only except emergencies; usually no referrals; priced between HMO and PPO.
  • POS (point of service): hybrid — HMO-like in-network with PCP referrals, plus partial out-of-network coverage at higher cost.

In-network providers accept the plan's negotiated rate; out-of-network providers don't — for non-emergency care you can owe the difference (balance billing) [S2]. The No Surprises Act removes that risk for emergencies and out-of-network clinicians at in-network facilities [S6]; Chapter 8 covers it.

Check your understanding: $0 deductible with high premiums, or $6,000 deductible with low premiums — which is cheaper?

It depends on usage. Healthy year: high-deductible usually wins. Bad year — surgery, a hospital stay: the low-deductible plan can, because its out-of-pocket maximum is lower. Run the sliders both ways to see the crossover.

CHAPTER 05 · WHO PAYS

The payers: employer plans, the Marketplace, Medicare, Medicaid, CHIP, VA and the uninsured

Everyone pays through one of these seven doors — or the gap between them.

Job with an employer — the largest single source

Employer-sponsored insurance covers the most nonelderly Americans. Employer and worker usually split the premium, typically pre-tax. Large employers usually self-fund — paying claims themselves and hiring a TPA — while smaller ones buy fully insured policies. COBRA extends job-based coverage after you leave, at your own expense.

The ACA Marketplace — individual coverage

Self-employed, unemployed or no affordable job offer: the Marketplace sells ACA-compliant individual plans with income-based premium tax credits and Silver cost-sharing reductions during annual open enrollment or life-event windows [S3]. Enhanced credits (2021–2025) buffered post-pandemic Medicaid losses; expiration raises subsidized premiums (KFF) [S19].

Medicare — federal insurance for older adults and some disabled people

  • Part A — hospital: inpatient, skilled nursing, hospice. Premium-free for most who worked enough quarters.
  • Part B — medical: doctor visits, outpatient care, preventive services, durable medical equipment. Premium, deductible, usually 20% coinsurance.
  • Part C — Medicare Advantage: private bundled A/B/D plans with networks — chosen by over half of beneficiaries [S20].
  • Part D — drugs: private plans with an out-of-pocket cap ($2,000 in 2025; $2,100 in 2026) [S21].
  • Medigap — private supplements filling Original Medicare gaps; cannot pair with Medicare Advantage.

Missing Part B's initial window risks lifetime late penalties [S4]. Unlike traditional Medicare, Medicare Advantage uses prior authorization broadly — covering 40% of Part B physician spending and 93% of physician-administered drugs at a major insurer (Penn LDI / BMJ) [S22, S23].

Medicaid and CHIP — state-run coverage for low income

Medicaid is joint federal–state insurance: states design and run programs within federal rules, so eligibility varies. 41 states (incl. D.C.) expanded eligibility to adults under 138% of poverty while 10 have not [S19]. CHIP covers children whose families earn too much for Medicaid but too little for private coverage [S5].

VA and TRICARE — coverage for service

The VA health system runs its own clinics and hospitals — government as both payer and provider; TRICARE covers active-duty members, families and many retirees. VA coordinates with Medicare rather than duplicating it [S10].

The uninsured and the underinsured

26.7 million people (7.9%) went uninsured all of 2025 (Census) [S18]. Under-65 uninsurance is 9.8% — 14.5% in non-expansion states vs. 8.0% in expansion states; 61.7% cite cost, and uninsured adults skip care over twice as often (38.6% vs. 17.0% private / 18.8% public) [S19]. Emergency stabilization is legally required, though bills still arrive (financial assistance can cut them). The underinsured rely on the out-of-pocket maximum in bad years.

Medical debt and charity care gaps

High deductibles and uninsurance leave Americans with at least $220 billion in medical debt: roughly 14 million adults owe over $1,000 and 3 million owe over $10,000, led by emergency room (50%) and inpatient hospital (35%) bills [S44, S45]. Even after credit bureaus voluntarily stopped reporting paid medical collections, debts under $500, and debts less than a year old, 15 million Americans still carried $49 billion in medical collections on credit reports in 2024 (averaging over $3,100) [S46]. Nonprofit hospitals must maintain financial assistance policies for tax exemption, yet charity care averages 1.4% or less of operating expenses at half of hospitals, and eligible patients are still routinely billed and sent to collections [S45, S46].

Check your understanding: Does Medicare cover long-term nursing home care?

Generally no. Medicare covers short-term skilled nursing after a qualifying hospital stay, not custodial long-term care. Medicaid is the main public payer, with strict income and asset rules.

CHAPTER 06 · ONE VISIT

A doctor visit, from scheduling to the bill

A routine visit is a short, predictable machine: schedule, check in, examine, code, claim, EOB, bill. Know the gears, save money and nerve.

Before you walk in

  • Scheduling. Front desk checks appointment, ID and eligibility; errors here (wrong ID, lapsed coverage) top denials.
  • Referrals. PCP order for a specialist; required by HMOs and many POS plans (no referral, no coverage), not PPOs or EPOs.
  • Prior authorization. Advance plan approval for costly imaging, drugs or electives — a coverage gate, not a clinical opinion, and never required in emergencies. Clinicians spend ~2 business days/week on approvals [S22]; a 2024 CMS rule (mostly 2027) sets 7-day standard / 72-hour urgent deadlines, denial reasons and electronic interfaces, with a 2026 proposal for drugs [S24].
  • Check-in. Insurance card, photo ID, demographics and copay.

The visit itself

The clinician examines you, orders tests or treatment and documents it in the medical record — not written down, not billed.

After you leave

  • Coding. A coder maps the note to ICD-10-CM diagnosis and CPT procedure codes (Chapter 7).
  • Claim and adjudication. Sent via a clearinghouse, the insurer applies the negotiated allowed amount and your cost sharing.
  • Paper trail. Within weeks, an Explanation of Benefits (EOB) arrives first (not a bill), then the provider's bill matching it line for line.

PRACTICAL CHECKLIST

Before: verify in-network status, prior authorization, copay and expected codes for costly care. After: match the EOB to the bill and dispute mismatches before paying.

Check your understanding: The ER says you need a non-emergency MRI in two weeks. What should you check first?

Prior authorization and whether the imaging center is in-network; missing either can leave you with full charges.

CHAPTER 07 · PAPER TRAIL

Billing and claims: codes, rates and the EOB decoded

A bill is the visible tip of a claims machine. Read the machine — codes, allowed amounts, the EOB — and the paperwork stops being intimidating.

The two code systems

  • ICD-10-CM (diagnosis). Why you were seen — S52.501A: unspecified lower-radius fracture, initial encounter. The diagnosis justifies everything billed after it.
  • CPT (procedure). What was done — 73100: wrist X-ray, single view; 99213: mid-complexity office visit. Maintained by the American Medical Association; related HCPCS codes cover supplies, ambulance rides and drugs.
  • Codes are paired: diagnosis justifies procedure. A wrist X-ray under a back-pain diagnosis fails a coverage edit — not because the X-ray was wrong.

Chargemaster versus the allowed amount

Hospitals keep a chargemaster — sticker prices that anchor uninsured bills, though almost nobody insured pays them. In-network contracts set the allowed amount; the gap — $4,530.00 of $9,500.00 — is written off, never balance-billed to an in-network patient. Federal price-transparency rules since 2020 exposed wide negotiated spreads (e.g., $79 vs. $613 for one MRI), yet raw files remain too unwieldy for patient shopping (Penn LDI / Wharton) [S25]. Enforcement lagged too: HHS OIG estimated 46% of hospitals were noncompliant, and GAO flagged incomplete machine-readable files—prompting CMS's 2026 rule requiring hospitals to publish 10th, median, and 90th percentile allowed amounts derived from 12–15 months of payment records alongside organizational NPIs [S52, S53, S54].

HOW TO READ ANY EOB IN 30 SECONDS

1) Find the allowed amount — the real price. 2) See how much went to your deductible. 3) Check the coinsurance math on the rest. 4) Plan payment + your share = allowed? 5) Match the EOB against the provider's bill — they must agree. Mismatch? The plan, then the provider.

The clearinghouse and the five gates

Most claims pass through a clearinghouse — validating file format, fixing obvious errors, routing. The insurer runs five adjudication gates in order: (1) patient eligible, coverage active; (2) covered benefit; (3) provider in-network; (4) medically necessary for the billed ICD-10 diagnosis; (5) cost sharing after deductible and coinsurance. Fail a gate, the claim pays zero — or less.

The full claim lifecycle: forms, DRGs and the 835 ERA

Behind Maya's EOB sits a standardized five-step billing pipeline linking clinical charts to your mailbox:

  1. Charge capture & coding. Chart notes convert into billable services priced off the chargemaster and paired with ICD-10-CM diagnoses plus CPT/HCPCS or hospital revenue codes [S47].
  2. Claim format: CMS-1500 (837P) vs. UB-04 / CMS-1450 (837I). Physicians, ambulances, and outpatient clinics send professional claims on CMS-1500 (electronic X12 837P) with POS codes and Type 1/Type 2 NPIs [S35, S42, S48]. Hospitals send facility charges on UB-04 (CMS-1450) (electronic X12 837I) grouped by NUBC revenue codes [S47, S49].
  3. Outpatient lines vs. inpatient MS-DRGs. Outpatient facility care pays per procedure group or line item, whereas Medicare and most payers bundle an entire inpatient hospital stay into a single MS-DRG (768 severity-adjusted diagnosis-related groups in FY 2027) based on principal diagnosis, comorbidities/complications, and procedures—while hospital doctors still bill separately on CMS-1500 [S50].
  4. Scrubbing, adjudication & X12 835 ERA. After clearinghouse scrubbing and the five payer gates, the insurer wires EFT funds for the plan's share and sends an 835 Electronic Remittance Advice carrying Claim Adjustment Reason Codes (CARCs) and Remark Codes (RARCs) that distinguish contractual write-offs from patient cost sharing [S47, S51].
  5. EOB & patient statement. The plan mails you the EOB; only after the provider posts the 835 ERA payment and contractual write-off should it send a patient statement for the verified deductible, copay, or coinsurance [S47, S51].

CHAPTER 08 · WHEN THINGS GO WRONG

Denials and appeals — and the No Surprises Act

A denial is a negotiating position, not a verdict. Plans deny routinely and reverse often on appeal; an independent referee exists. Here's how to use it.

Why claims get denied

  • Coding mismatch. Diagnosis and procedure codes do not pair, or a code is missing digits. Fixed by resubmission.
  • Missing prior authorization. Advance approval never obtained (or obtained for the wrong code). Often appealable if care was urgent.
  • Not medically necessary. Plan policy does not accept the treatment as necessary. The classic internal-appeal target, argued with physician letters.
  • Not a covered benefit. Excluded outright — cosmetic care, experimental therapy, routine adult dental. Usually final.
  • Eligibility / coverage lapsed. The plan shows you were not covered on the date of service — often an employer data error; fixable with proof.
  • Duplicate or unbundled claim. Billed twice, or components that must be billed together were split. Chapter 11's ER physician claim started here.
  • Out-of-network, no authorization. Non-emergency, non-protected care may cost you more. Where the No Surprises Act changes the rules.

Your appeal rights

  • Denial and appeal rates (2024). HealthCare.gov plans denied 19% of in-network claims (3%–36% across insurers) and 37% out-of-network; <1% were appealed, with 66% upheld [S26]. Medicare Advantage denied 7.7% (4.1M) of prior auths; 11.5% were appealed, and 80.7% were overturned on appeal [S20]. Across all insured adults, 69% view prior authorization as a burden (34% call it their top non-cost barrier), 47% experienced delayed or denied care over two years (57% with chronic conditions), and 1 in 4 affected adults reported a major negative health impact [S55].
  • Denial letter & internal appeal. Group plans allow at least 180 days [S8]; appeal in writing with records and a clinician's necessity letter (30 days pre-service, 60 post-service, 72 hours urgent).
  • External review & regulators. An independent reviewer's ruling binds the plan. Job plans: DOL EBSA [S8]; individual: state insurance department; Medicare: 1-800-MEDICARE.

Surprise billing and the No Surprises Act

A surprise bill is a balance bill from an out-of-network clinician you could not choose — like an anesthesiologist you never met. Since 2022, the No Surprises Act bars these in three cases [S6, S7]:

  • Emergency services anywhere, without prior authorization, at in-network cost sharing.
  • Non-emergency care at in-network facilities by out-of-network clinicians (anesthesia, radiology, pathology, assistant surgeons).
  • Out-of-network air ambulances.

Self-pay or uninsured patients get a good faith estimate before scheduled care and can dispute bills $400+ above it [S6]. Ground ambulances remain excluded federally (24 states regulate them) [S29].

Five-year evidence: A 2025 BMJ study found an 18.0% differential out-of-pocket drop (~$567/yr) in newly protected states [S27]. Providers win >80% of federal Independent Dispute Resolution (IDR) payment arbitrations at rates well above median in-network prices, prompting a 2026 rule tightening eligibility (Penn LDI) [S28, S29]. Help Desk: 1-800-985-3059 [S6].

WORKED EXAMPLE · THE ER PHYSICIAN DENIAL

Chapter 11's episode: the out-of-network ER physician's claim was denied as a duplicate of the facility claim. The provider corrected and resubmitted; refused again, Maya's clinic filed an internal appeal with the operative note. The plan reversed the denial and paid $560.00; Maya's share stayed $140.00. Nothing about the care changed — only the paperwork.

Check your understanding: A $3,200 bill from an out-of-network radiologist arrives two months after your in-network colonoscopy. Your rights?

The No Surprises Act caps your share at in-network cost sharing — radiology at an in-network facility is protected, no valid waiver. Pay the in-network share on your EOB, send the provider the EOB, call the No Surprises Help Desk (1-800-985-3059) if they persist. Their claimed exception is their burden to prove.

CHAPTER 09 · DRUGS

Prescription drugs: pharmacies, PBMs, formularies and why prices vary

Every pill has two prices — the one on the bottle and the one behind it. The PBM chain explains the difference.

Brand, generic and biosimilar

  • Brand-name drugs are the innovator's patented product — usually expensive, tiered high on a formulary.
  • Generic drugs hold the same active ingredient at the same dose, FDA-verified equivalent — same effect, a fraction of the price. The highest-confidence way to cut a drug bill.
  • Biosimilars are the biologic equivalent: highly similar, not identical, cheaper copies of complex protein drugs.

The PBM's job — and the controversy

A pharmacy benefit manager (PBM) runs drug benefits for plans and employers: it builds the formulary, sets tier cost sharing, negotiates rebates and pays pharmacies. Three PBMs—CVS Caremark (34%), Express Scripts (23%), and OptumRx (22%)—manage 79% of prescriptions (drugs are ~10–14% of spending) [S12, S30, S56]; Part D insurer–PBM mergers (30% to 80%) yielded no member savings and raised rival premiums (Penn LDI, 2026) [S31].

FTC audits documented two hidden revenue streams: PBMs steered 68% of specialty revenue to affiliated pharmacies, marking up 51 specialty generics by $7.3 billion above acquisition cost, and pocketed $1.4 billion via spread pricing (billing plans more than pharmacies received, 2017–2022) [S57, S60]. To curb fee and broker opacity, federal ERISA rules now mandate PBM and broker compensation disclosure for large self-insured plans [S8, S61], and the Consolidated Appropriations Act of 2026 delinks Part D PBM compensation from drug list prices starting 2028 while requiring 100% rebate pass-through to ERISA employer plans [S56].

Rebates, the gross-to-net bubble and Medicare negotiation

Manufacturers set a public list price and pay confidential post-sale rebates to PBMs in exchange for preferred formulary placement—creating a widening gross-to-net bubble [S56, S57]. For top insulins (2012–2019), gross sales more than doubled ($13B to $27B) while net sales dropped ~40% ($8B to $5B) as concessions exceeded 80%, before manufacturers cut list prices 65%–80% in 2023–2024 [S58]. Because deductibles and coinsurance (paid by 57.3% of commercial patients across 79 brand drugs) track the inflated list price rather than the confidential net price, sick patients subsidize lower plan premiums [S56, S59]. Dominant drugmakers also defend market share with rebate walls—volume contracts conditioning discounts on excluding lower-list-price rivals or biosimilars [S56, S57].

Under the 2022 Inflation Reduction Act, Medicare drug price negotiation authorizes CMS to set transparent Maximum Fair Prices for top single-source drugs lacking generic or biosimilar competition, replacing secret Part D rebates [S62, S63]. Across the first three cycles (2026–2028)—40 drugs representing 36% ($125 billion) of gross Medicare drug spending—negotiated prices cut Cycle 1 (2026, 10 Part D drugs) by 22% and Cycle 2 (2027, 15 Part D drugs) by 44% below prior net prices, with Cycle 3 (2028) expanding to physician-administered Part B drugs [S62, S63].

WORKED EXAMPLE · LIST PRICE VS. NET PRICE AT THE COUNTER

Suppose an illustrative brand drug carries a $500.00 list price and a 50% ($250.00) confidential rebate to the PBM, leaving a $250.00 net cost. A patient in a 20% coinsurance phase owes 20% of the $500.00 list price ($100.00) at the pharmacy counter—40% of the actual $250.00 net price—while the $250.00 rebate flows back to the plan later [S56, S59].

Why the same drug costs different amounts

  1. List vs net. List is public; net after rebates is private.
  2. Tier placement. Preferred generic ($0–$10) up to specialty.
  3. Formulary rules. Exclusions, prior authorization or step therapy (cheapest first).
  4. Channel. Retail, mail or specialty; manufacturer coupons exclude Medicare/Medicaid.
  5. Site of care. Infused drugs often cost less in clinics than hospital departments — different codes, different fees.

Numbers a beginner should recognize

Three anchors: (1) Medicare Part D out-of-pocket spending is capped at $2,000 in 2025 and $2,100 in 2026; Medicare insulin is $35/month [S21]. (2) ACA plans cover recommended preventive vaccines at $0. (3) Cash discount programs make many generics ~$4 — compare both before paying your copay.

Check your understanding: A brand drug costs $180/month on your plan; the generic is $8. What is the catch?

Usually none — generics are FDA-verified as therapeutically equivalent. Exceptions: a narrow-therapeutic-index drug where the prescriber insists on one manufacturer, or a prior-authorization wrinkle. If substitution is fine, it is the biggest savings action on the list.

CHAPTER 10 · THE OTHER HALF

Public health and population health

Most of your health was decided before you felt anything. Public health works on communities, not individuals — the cheapest medicine there is.

Who does what

  • Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). The national public health agency: surveillance (NREVSS, PulseNet), immunization schedules, the MMWR, travel health, lab reference [S11].
  • State health departments. License facilities and clinicians, run immunization registries, investigate outbreaks, manage vital statistics, administer Medicaid with CMS and WIC with USDA.
  • Local and tribal health departments. Restaurant and water inspection, lead abatement, contact tracing, vaccine clinics — the boots-on-the-ground layer.
  • Other federal players. FDA approves drugs and devices; NIH funds research; CMS pays for care, sets hospital quality and emergency standards.

Vaccination and screening: what "population health" buys

Vaccines build herd immunity, shielding those who cannot be vaccinated; slipping uptake cost the U.S. its measles elimination status in 2026 (Penn LDI) [S32]. Screening catches cancer, high blood pressure and newborn disorders early, when treatment is cheapest [S9].

How it differs from personal medical care

  • Unit & goal. Population or place prevention vs. one patient's diagnosis and treatment.
  • Payment & evidence. Taxes/grants and outbreak surveillance vs. claims and clinical trials.
  • Failure mode. A missed outbreak signal caught weeks late vs. a missed diagnosis.

Why that broader layer matters: U.S. child and adolescent mortality has run ~2× peer OECD nations' rates since 1975 (~45 excess deaths/day), driven largely by conditions outside clinics (Penn LDI / Pediatrics, 2026) [S33].

WORKED EXAMPLE · ONE BROKEN ARM AS A PUBLIC-HEALTH DATA POINT

Maya's broken arm looks like one bill — but EDs report syndromic data to public health systems. If dozens of identical ice-related broken arms hit the same week, local health departments see it before the news does — signage, sidewalk treatment, school closures. Personal care fixes Maya's arm; population health prevents the next hundred broken arms.

Check your understanding: Your neighbor has measles symptoms. Is your pediatrician paid to handle this, or is someone else involved?

Both. Your pediatrician treats the patient; the health department is notified the same day — measles is reportable — and investigates contacts and containment. Reportable diseases are the rare case where the two halves share a clipboard.

The denial and appeal, exactly

The ER physician's $700.00-allowed claim was denied as a duplicate of the facility claim. The provider corrected and resubmitted; refused again, Maya's clinic filed an internal appeal within the 180-day window with the operative note. The plan reversed the denial and paid $560.00; her share stayed $140.00 — exactly what she would have owed had it paid cleanly. Appeals change who pays whom, not the price. The No Surprises Act also barred balance-billing her the other $400.00 above allowed.

What changes with a different plan

  • Uninsured. Maya would face the $9,500.00 chargemaster, though she could ask for a good faith estimate and financial assistance; bills ≥$400 above the estimate are disputable [S6].
  • High-deductible plan. With a $3,000.00 deductible unmet, she would owe every allowed dollar — $4,970.00 — and the insurer would pay $0.00 this episode.
  • OOP maximum reached earlier. She would owe nothing further; the plan pays 100% of allowed in-network charges.
  • No Surprises Act edge cases. Protection covers the ER physician and air ambulance — but ground ambulance balance billing can still happen in many states (24 states have passed state-level protections); ask whether a non-emergency ride is in-network [S6, S29].
Final check: Maya's friend says "the hospital made $4,530.00 in free discounts — so the system isn't really expensive." Right frame?

The write-off is the contract, not generosity: the hospital agreed $4,970.00 settles the claim in full for in-network patients. The real cost lives in the total — premiums ($5,760.00/yr, Chapter 4), cost sharing ($1,794.00), employer contributions and taxes. Bills are the visible edge of a $5.3-trillion machine [S12].

Glossary

Short, plain-language definitions cross-checked against HealthCare.gov's glossary [S2]. Meanings vary by plan, state and circumstance.

Payer
Whoever pays for care: an insurer, a self-funding employer, or the government.
CMS
The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services — runs Medicare, Medicaid's federal side and the Marketplace; writes payment and quality rules.
Affordable Care Act (ACA)
The 2010 law that created the Marketplaces, premium tax credits and insurance-market rules, and expanded Medicaid.
Marketplace (exchange)
The regulated market for ACA-compliant individual plans with income-based subsidies; HealthCare.gov or a state-run site.
Emergency medical services (EMS)
911-dispatched ambulance crews — EMTs and paramedics — who stabilize and transport.
Emergency department (ED / ER)
The hospital's 24/7 front door. Triage sorts by urgency; the sickest go first.
Triage
Sorting patients by urgency so the most unstable are treated first, not first-come.
Urgent care
Walk-in same-day care for problems too urgent for a scheduled visit, but not life- or limb-threatening.
Primary care
Ongoing, first-contact care: checkups, common illness, chronic disease, referrals. Family medicine, internal medicine, pediatrics.
Specialist
A clinician focused on an organ system or service — cardiology, orthopedics, dermatology. May need referral in HMO and POS plans.
Clinic
Any ambulatory care site: physician practices, retail clinics, community health centers, hospital outpatient departments.
Hospital outpatient department (HOPD)
An outpatient clinic owned by and billed through a hospital (POS 22 on campus, POS 19 off campus), generating both a professional claim and a hospital facility fee.
Facility fee
A separate hospital or surgical-center charge covering nursing, rooms, equipment and overhead—added on top of the clinician's professional bill.
Site-neutral payment
Paying the same rate for the same medical service regardless of whether a clinic is independent or owned by a hospital.
Vertical integration
When hospitals or insurers acquire physician practices, outpatient centers, or pharmacies across different stages of care.
Private equity (PE) ownership
Investment funds that acquire medical practices—often via roll-ups—to restructure billing, volume, and payer contracts before reselling.
Telehealth
Medical care delivered remotely by video or phone instead of an in-person office visit.
Inpatient
Formally admitted to a hospital bed. Admission status changes billing, drug charges, sometimes your out-of-pocket maximum.
Outpatient
Treated and released the same day — clinic visits, most surgery, most imaging.
Observation status
Monitored in a hospital bed without admission. Bills like outpatient care — sometimes costlier for drugs.
Physician (MD / DO)
A doctor licensed by a state board after college, medical school and residency. DOs emphasize whole-person, osteopathic manipulative technique.
Registered nurse (RN)
Assesses, medicates, runs care plans, coordinates the team. Licensed by state boards after training and the NCLEX-RN.
NCLEX-RN
The standardized exam every US RN must pass; NCLEX-PN separately covers practical nurses.
Nurse practitioner (NP)
An RN with graduate training who diagnoses, treats and prescribes, often in primary care. Authority varies by state.
Physician assistant (PA)
Medical-model clinician with a roughly 2–3-year master's; diagnoses, treats and prescribes under state-defined supervision or collaboration.
EMT (emergency medical technician)
Front-line 911 clinician: airway, CPR, bleeding control, splints, short approved medication list. Certificate training, months.
Paramedic
Advanced 911 clinician: IVs, advanced airways, cardiac monitoring, wider drug formulary. Roughly 1–2 years of training after EMT.
Pharmacist
Doctor of pharmacy (PharmD): dispenses medicines, verifies interactions and dosing, and counsels patients. State license required.
Technician / assistant
Support roles: phlebotomists (blood draws), radiology technologists (imaging), pharmacy and lab technicians, nursing assistants. Usually certificate programs plus an exam.
Scope of practice
What a clinician is legally allowed to do, set by state law. It changes — check your state's board.
Credentialing and privileges
Beyond a state license, hospitals verify training and grant "privileges" — permission to perform specific procedures there.
National Provider Identifier (NPI)
A unique 10-digit HIPAA ID assigned by CMS (Type 1 for individual clinicians, Type 2 for organizations) required on standard claims.
Taxonomy code
A 10-character NUCC alphanumeric code identifying a clinician's or facility's specialty and classification on enrollment and claims.
Premium
The fixed monthly price of coverage, paid whether or not you use care. Job-based plans usually split it, pre-tax.
Deductible
What you pay for covered services each plan year before the plan starts sharing costs. Resets annually.
Copayment (copay)
A fixed dollar amount for a service, usually at the time of care — e.g., $25 primary visit, $250 ER.
Coinsurance
Your percentage share of the allowed amount after the deductible — 20% of $700.00 is $140.00.
Out-of-pocket maximum
Annual ceiling on in-network cost sharing. Hit it and the plan pays 100% of covered in-network allowed costs; premiums never count.
Allowed amount
The negotiated price for a covered in-network service — the number the plan splits with you. Charges above it are written off, never billed.
HMO
Health maintenance organization: contracted network only (emergencies excepted), typically a primary-care gatekeeper and referral requirement; lower premiums.
PPO
Broad network, no referrals; cheaper in-network, costlier (or uncovered) out-of-network; higher premiums.
EPO
Exclusive provider organization: in-network only (emergencies excepted), usually no referrals; priced between HMO and PPO.
POS
Point of service: HMO-like care with a primary-care gatekeeper in-network, plus partial out-of-network coverage at higher cost.
Primary care physician (PCP)
Your ongoing clinician and, in HMO and POS plans, the referral gatekeeper.
Referral
Your PCP's order for specialist care. Required in HMO and many POS plans; not in PPO/EPO.
In-network
Providers who signed the plan's rate contract. You pay only cost sharing; balance billing is not allowed.
Out-of-network
Providers without a rate contract. Non-emergency OON care can mean full charges plus balance billing; emergencies are protected by the No Surprises Act.
Balance billing
Billing you the charge minus the plan's allowed amount — banned in-network and under the No Surprises Act.
Actuarial value
Rough share of average allowed costs a plan pays — Bronze ≈60%, Silver ≈70%, Gold ≈80%, Platinum ≈90%. Not a quality rating.
Employer-sponsored insurance (ESI)
Health coverage through a job, usually with the employer sharing the premium; the largest source of US coverage.
Self-funded plan
The employer pays claims itself rather than buying insurance; usually run by a third-party administrator (ERISA).
Third-party administrator (TPA)
The company that runs a self-funded plan's paperwork — claims, networks, ID cards — without bearing the insurance risk.
Fully insured plan
The employer buys an insurance policy; the insurer bears the claim risk and is state-regulated.
COBRA
Federal right to keep employer coverage temporarily after job loss — usually at your full cost plus 2%.
Premium tax credit
Income-based tax credit that lowers Marketplace premiums; paid monthly in advance or claimed at filing.
Cost-sharing reduction (CSR)
Extra help lowering deductibles and copays on Silver Marketplace plans for lower incomes.
Medicare Advantage (Part C)
Private plans bundling Parts A, B (usually D), often with extra benefits and networks, paid a capitated rate.
Medigap
Standardized private supplements filling Original Medicare cost-sharing gaps. Not combinable with Medicare Advantage.
Medicaid
Joint federal–state insurance for low-income people. States administer within federal rules; eligibility and benefits vary by state.
CHIP
Children's Health Insurance Program: coverage for children in families above Medicaid income limits but below private affordability.
TRICARE
The Department of Defense program for active-duty members, their families and many retirees.
VA health care
Veterans' care delivered directly by the Department of Veterans Affairs through its own hospitals and clinics.
Uninsured
No coverage of any kind. Emergency stabilization is required regardless of payment ability; the bill still comes.
Underinsured
Covered on paper, but deductibles and out-of-pocket maximums exceed what the household can absorb.
Financial assistance (charity care)
Hospital and plan programs reducing or forgiving bills for low-income patients; nonprofit hospitals must maintain such policies.
Medical debt
Unpaid out-of-pocket medical bills owed to providers or third-party collectors; often triggered by emergency care, hospitalizations, or unmet deductibles.
Prior authorization (prior auth)
Advance plan approval that a service, drug or device is necessary and covered — a coverage gate, not a clinical judgment.
Medical record
The clinical record of your care — the chart your bills are built from.
ICD-10-CM
The diagnosis codes: why you were seen. S52.501A is an unspecified lower-radius fracture, initial encounter.
CPT
Current Procedural Terminology — what was done. 73100 is a wrist X-ray, single view.
HCPCS
Codes for what CPT doesn't cover: supplies, ambulance, drugs, DME — Level II (A-codes, J-codes).
Clearinghouse
A switch between providers and payers: validates file format, fixes routine errors, routes claims.
Charge capture
Translating documented clinical care into coded, billable line items priced from the chargemaster before claim submission.
CMS-1500 (X12 837P)
The standard paper form (and electronic 837P format) used by physicians, clinics, and ambulance suppliers to bill professional services.
UB-04 / CMS-1450 (X12 837I)
The standard institutional claim form (and electronic 837I format) used by hospitals and facilities, organized by NUBC revenue codes.
MS-DRG (Diagnosis-Related Group)
A single bundled payment category for an inpatient hospital stay based on principal diagnosis, comorbidities, procedures, and severity.
Electronic Remittance Advice (X12 835)
The insurer's machine-readable payment notice to a provider—using CARC and RARC codes to record paid amounts, contractual write-offs, and patient responsibility.
Patient statement
The provider's bill sent to you after insurer adjudication and 835 ERA posting, requesting your deductible, copay, or coinsurance balance.
Explanation of Benefits (EOB)
The insurer's itemized statement of allowed amount, deductible, plan payment and your share. Not a bill.
Write-off (adjustment)
Charge minus allowed that a contracted provider gives up; may not bill it to you in-network.
Chargemaster
The hospital's internal list of sticker prices. Rarely the real price; anchors the uninsured bill.
External review
Independent re-adjudication after an internal appeal fails. Binding on the plan if it upholds you.
Surprise bill
A balance bill from an out-of-network clinician you could not meaningfully choose — now restricted by the No Surprises Act.
No Surprises Act
2022 federal law: emergency OON care, OON clinicians at in-network facilities and air ambulances must be treated as in-network.
Independent Dispute Resolution (IDR)
Federal arbitration under the No Surprises Act where an outside arbiter chooses between the insurer's and provider's payment offers without raising the patient's share.
Good faith estimate
For uninsured or self-pay patients, a written estimate of expected charges; bills ≥$400 over it are disputable.
Pharmacy benefit manager (PBM)
The administrator of the drug benefit — formulary design, rebate negotiation, pharmacy reimbursement — for plans and employers.
Formulary
The drugs a plan covers, with tiers and any prior-authorization or step-therapy rules.
Drug tier
Cost-sharing rung in the formulary — preferred generic, generic, brand, preferred brand, specialty.
Rebate
Manufacturer-to-PBM money for formulary placement — a list-price discount invisible at the counter.
Gross-to-net bubble
The widening gap between a drug's public list price and its confidential net price after PBM rebates and statutory discounts—inflating list-linked coinsurance.
Rebate wall
Volume or exclusivity manufacturer contracts that condition large rebates on excluding lower-list-price competitors or biosimilars from preferred formulary tiers.
Medicare drug price negotiation (IRA)
Authority under the 2022 Inflation Reduction Act allowing CMS to set transparent Maximum Fair Prices for top single-source Part D and Part B drugs.
Spread pricing
A PBM charges the plan more than it pays the pharmacy and keeps the difference.
Brand-name drug
The innovator's patented product. Expensive; high tiers; often first authorized as generic after patent expiry.
Generic drug
FDA-approved equivalent of a brand drug: same active ingredient, strength and route. Far cheaper.
Biosimilar
A highly similar, non-identical copy of a biologic — approved safe and effective, priced below the reference product.
CDC
The national public health agency — surveillance, immunization, outbreak response.
MMWR
The CDC's weekly bulletin of public health findings and outbreak reports.
Vaccine
A preparation that trains the immune system against disease — the foundation of population-level protection.
Screening
Testing before symptoms — cancer, blood pressure, cholesterol, newborns — when treatment is cheapest.
