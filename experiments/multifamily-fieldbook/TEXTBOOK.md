## Chapter A · Who decides what?

Take the same parcel to a planner, an assessor and a lender. Each can answer a different question. Start by learning which desk to call.

### A1. A rent benchmark is not a lease

The U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD) runs federal housing programs. Its Fair Market Rent (FMR) is an area and bedroom specific estimate of gross monthly rent used in rental assistance. Gross here includes eligible tenant-paid utilities.

**Why it matters.** An investor asks what the owner can collect. A public benchmark describes a market for a program; it is not a signed rent, a property appraisal or a guaranteed voucher payment.

**Illustrative worked example.** If a two-bedroom FMR is $1,600 and the comparable utility allowance is $100, compare $1,500 to the owner’s contract rent. Do not put $1,600 per unit into the revenue line just because it came from HUD.

**At the product desk.** For River North, ask what supports the rent assumption and which unit mix it covers. The brief supplies neither leases nor a rent roll. It does not say the app ingests FMRs.

**Meeting mistake.** “HUD says these units will rent for $1,600.” A lease, current comparable leases and concessions are the evidence you need.

**Drill down.** HUD also designates Qualified Census Tracts (QCTs) and Difficult Development Areas (DDAs) for the Low-Income Housing Tax Credit (LIHTC). A QCT concerns local income or poverty; a DDA concerns high development costs relative to incomes. These can matter to eligible tax credit basis. HUD maps can put federal layers side by side; the Treasury Department, not HUD, certifies Opportunity Zones. [VERIFY] Use HUD’s lists for the relevant year, not a screenshot.

**Primary checks:** HUD USER · Fair Market Rents; HUD USER · Qualified Census Tracts and Difficult Development Areas. See the verification desk.

**Quiz — answer before reading the key**

1. Recall: Explain fair market rent in one sentence.
2. Apply: What would support a proposed rent at River North?
3. Investor, 30 seconds: Why not use one official rent for every unit?

**Answer key.** Recall: The U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD) runs federal housing programs. Its Fair Market Rent (FMR) is an area and bedroom specific estimate of gross monthly rent used in rental assistance. Gross here includes eligible tenant-paid utilities. Apply: Comparable recent leases for similar units, adjusted for utilities and concessions. A benchmark starts a conversation; leases with matching unit sizes and terms support the assumption. Investor example: “HUD gives us a comparable benchmark, not this building’s leases. I would check bedroom mix, utilities and signed comparables before defending a rent.”

**Add to your glossary:**

- HUD: U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development; federal housing agency.
- FMR: Fair Market Rent; gross rent benchmark by place and bedroom count.
- LIHTC: Low-Income Housing Tax Credit; a federal affordable rental housing credit generally allocated through state housing agencies.
- QCT: Qualified Census Tract; an income or poverty based LIHTC designation.
- DDA: Difficult Development Area; a cost relative to income LIHTC designation.

### A2. Follow the Treasury data trail

The Community Development Financial Institutions (CDFI) Fund is part of the U.S. Treasury. It certifies qualifying mission-driven financial institutions, administers New Markets Tax Credit (NMTC) allocations and published resources for the original Opportunity Zone (OZ) tract designations.

**Why it matters.** A tract list, a lender certification and a tax credit allocation are different outputs. None of them is a commitment to lend on your building.

**Illustrative worked example.** Two sites may lie in the same designated tract. A lender willing to finance one has made zero commitments to the other. Name the institution, the program and the written commitment separately.

**At the product desk.** Use the Treasury tract identifier and designation vintage as the evidence behind the OZ overlay. Do not infer that a CDFI is involved in River North.

**Meeting mistake.** “This tract got an NMTC allocation.” The Fund allocates tax credit authority to qualified Community Development Entities, not automatically to every project on a map.

**Drill down.** For the original OZ round, state executives nominated eligible tracts and Treasury certified designations. HUD tools can display this geography. Congress sets the statute; Treasury and the Internal Revenue Service (IRS) implement it. This chain is why the map publisher, the certifier and the tax adviser are not interchangeable.

**Primary checks:** U.S. Treasury CDFI Fund · Opportunity Zones Resources; CDFI Fund · New Markets Tax Credit Program. See the verification desk.

**Quiz — answer before reading the key**

1. Recall: Explain the cdfi fund in one sentence.
2. Apply: What can the 2018 tract list establish for your map?
3. Investor, 30 seconds: Why keep the publisher next to a federal overlay?

**Answer key.** Recall: The Community Development Financial Institutions (CDFI) Fund is part of the U.S. Treasury. It certifies qualifying mission-driven financial institutions, administers New Markets Tax Credit (NMTC) allocations and published resources for the original Opportunity Zone (OZ) tract designations. Apply: Whether a mapped location overlaps a tract in that designation vintage. The list is evidence of geography, not financing or a tax opinion. Investor example: “A HUD map can display a Treasury designation. I want the investor to know whose tract list we used and what it does not certify.”

**Add to your glossary:**

- CDFI: Community Development Financial Institution; a certified mission-driven financial institution, distinct from the Treasury fund that certifies it.
- NMTC: New Markets Tax Credit; allocation authority awarded to qualifying Community Development Entities.
- OZ: Opportunity Zone; a Treasury-certified geographic designation used in an investment tax regime.

### A3. A tax boundary is only the first test

A Qualified Opportunity Fund (QOF) is an investment vehicle that must satisfy statutory asset tests. It may invest through a Qualified Opportunity Zone Business (QOZB), an operating entity subject to separate tests. A pin in an OZ is not either of those entities.

**Why it matters.** An investor’s eligible gain, investment timing, fund structure, property qualification and holding period all matter. Congress wrote Internal Revenue Code sections 1400Z-1 and 1400Z-2; Treasury regulations and IRS guidance explain administration.

**Illustrative worked example.** Suppose an investor reinvests $100 of eligible gain in a qualifying fund interest which later sells for $160. Deferring the original $100 and potentially excluding the subsequent $60 appreciation are two different benefits. A basis step-up raises the tax starting value used to measure a gain; it is not a cash payment.

**At the product desk.** Your 2018 overlay on 2010 tracts is a screening lead. Do not model a tax saving for River North without parcel confirmation, transaction dates and fund counsel’s analysis.

**Meeting mistake.** “Hold the building ten years and all tax disappears.” Under the original regime the qualifying holding period concerns the investment in the fund; the original deferred gain has a separate inclusion date.

**Drill down.** [VERIFY] For the original regime, compare an earlier inclusion event with December 31, 2026 for recognition of the deferred gain. Earlier five- and seven-year basis increases depended on having enough time before that date. After a qualifying ten-year hold, a fair-market-value basis election on sale of a qualifying fund interest can exclude eligible appreciation, not the original deferred gain. The 2025 changes often called “OZ 2.0” provide for recurring designation rounds. For qualifying investments made after 2026, check the rolling five-year deferral clock (or earlier inclusion), the five-year basis increase and enhanced treatment for qualifying rural funds. An ordinary rural address alone does not meet a fund-level test. Do not roll the legacy map forward or blend the clocks. Check Public Law 119-21 section 70421, the effective dates for the particular investment, current IRS guidance and the applicable Treasury designation list before quoting terms.

**Primary checks:** GovInfo · Public Law 119-21, section 70421; Internal Revenue Service · Opportunity Zones; U.S. Treasury CDFI Fund · Opportunity Zones Resources. See the verification desk.

**Quiz — answer before reading the key**

1. Recall: Explain an opportunity zone investment in one sentence.
2. Apply: A sponsor wants to put ten years of tax savings into the base case solely from a map hit. What do you do?
3. Investor, 30 seconds: Why show the zone but not promise a tax result?

**Answer key.** Recall: A Qualified Opportunity Fund (QOF) is an investment vehicle that must satisfy statutory asset tests. It may invest through a Qualified Opportunity Zone Business (QOZB), an operating entity subject to separate tests. A pin in an OZ is not either of those entities. Apply: Separate the geographic hit from the investment tests and request current tax analysis. A tract is only one condition. Structure, vintage, holding periods and elections need separate evidence. Investor example: “The boundary helps us find a question worth asking. I would verify the designation and have counsel test the specific investment before giving a tax benefit any value.”

**Add to your glossary:**

- IRS: Internal Revenue Service; administers federal tax law.
- QOF: Qualified Opportunity Fund; investment vehicle subject to qualifying asset rules.
- QOZB: Qualified Opportunity Zone Business; a business subject to separate qualifying requirements.
- Basis: Tax starting value used in calculating gain or loss, subject to adjustments.
- Deferral: Postponing recognition of a gain; not the same as excluding it.

### A4. Who stands behind agency debt?

The Federal Housing Finance Agency (FHFA) regulates Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, government-sponsored enterprises. Through lender networks, the enterprises buy or guarantee qualifying mortgages and help fund a secondary mortgage market. FHFA is not your loan officer.

**Why it matters.** A lender expecting to deliver a loan to an enterprise cares about its underwriting and documentation standards. A construction loan and a permanent mortgage on a leased building solve different problems.

**Illustrative worked example.** If a building produces $120,000 a year before debt and proposed annual debt payments are $100,000, there is $1.20 of income for each $1.00 owed. That is a coverage calculation, not an agency approval.

**At the product desk.** Keep the income period and source beside the model output. Ask for the actual lender term sheet; the brief does not establish a River North lender or an agency execution.

**Meeting mistake.** “Fannie and Freddie are HUD grants.” This is mortgage finance. Loan size, eligibility and terms still need underwriting.

**Drill down.** A broker may quote preliminary proceeds, an underwriter may adjust rents or reserves, and the lender may issue a conditional commitment. Those are three different levels of evidence. [VERIFY] Check the applicable enterprise multifamily guide and lender term sheet for live requirements; there is no universal coverage threshold in this book.

**Primary checks:** Federal Housing Finance Agency; Fannie Mae · Multifamily Selling and Servicing Guide; Freddie Mac · Multifamily. See the verification desk.

### A5. A state atlas is not the local code

Washington’s Growth Management Act (GMA) sets a planning framework. Comprehensive plans describe a jurisdiction’s long-term policy; development regulations implement it. The Washington State Department of Commerce publishes the Washington State Zoning Atlas (WAZA), a generalized research snapshot.

**Why it matters.** State middle housing laws can require local changes that allow more housing forms between detached homes and large apartment buildings. An atlas observation, a future land-use designation and today’s legally effective rules may differ.

**Illustrative worked example.** Suppose a plan calls for apartments in a corridor but the operative code still limits the parcel to a different use. Do not price apartment land on the plan alone. Check adopted amendments, effective dates and any directly applicable state rules.

**At the product desk.** Your snapshot spans April 2024 to June 2025. Label it “not a legal zoning determination.” Keep the native zone code, not just one of the four map classes.

### A6. Three questions, three local desks

### A7. Keep a tract with its vintage

## Chapter B · Read a site

Draw the site twice: once as the law describes it, once as a building that actually fits. The smaller answer usually sets the next question.

### B1. Count homes, then count rentable area

A dwelling unit is one home. Unit mix is the distribution of homes by type, such as studios and two-bedrooms. Gross building area includes circulation and shared areas; net rentable area counts space that can generate unit rent under the stated measurement convention.

**Why it matters.** Two 100-unit plans can have different construction costs, rents and household markets. Lenders want the schedule of unit types and areas, not only the headline count.

**Illustrative worked example.** Ten 600-square-foot units give 6,000 rentable square feet. If the building is 7,500 gross square feet, its net-to-gross efficiency is 6,000 ÷ 7,500 = 80%. Applying unit rent per square foot to the corridors overstates income.

**At the product desk.** River North has 180 units in the supplied summary and 200 in the model. Neither source gives a unit mix in your brief. Ask for the dated unit schedule; do not infer smaller units or a redesign.

**Meeting mistake.** “Twenty more units means twenty identical rents at no extra cost.” Check unit sizes, common areas, costs and achievable rents together.

**Drill down.** A broker may quote rent per rentable square foot while a contractor quotes cost per gross square foot. Keep both denominators and their measurement standards. Multiplying the two rates by the same floor area hides real circulation, stairs and services.

**Quiz — answer before reading the key**

1. Recall: Explain unit mix in one sentence.
2. Apply: Which additional River North document explains the difference between area and unit count?
3. Investor, 30 seconds: Why is unit count not enough for a rent projection?

**Answer key.** Recall: A dwelling unit is one home. Unit mix is the distribution of homes by type, such as studios and two-bedrooms. Gross building area includes circulation and shared areas; net rentable area counts space that can generate unit rent under the stated measurement convention. Apply: A dated unit and area schedule tied to a plan set. Match areas and counts to the same design revision. Investor example: “I need the mix and rentable areas to apply credible rents. A gross building area includes space the owner builds but cannot rent as apartments.”

**Add to your glossary:**

- Unit mix: Counts by unit type, often with areas and rents.
- Gross building area: Floor area including common and service space under a stated standard.
- Net rentable area: Area used to calculate rent under the stated convention.

### B2. Turn acres into a screening count

Dwelling units per acre (DU/acre) is a unit count divided by the site acreage specified in the code. A density ceiling is an arithmetic limit, not a site plan.

**Why it matters.** A minimum-unit filter is useful for discarding unlikely candidates. A developer still has to fit the units, satisfy use rules and obtain approvals.

**Illustrative worked example.** At an illustrative maximum of 40 DU/acre, 1.5 qualifying acres × 40 gives a 60-unit ceiling. A Min units filter set to 50 can keep this candidate; it has not proved 50 units fit.

**At the product desk.** Keep the field, acreage basis and research vintage behind the filter. When density is missing or the allowed use is unknown, distinguish unknown from zero and from a verified pass.

**Meeting mistake.** “The parcel is two acres on the county map, so all two acres count.” Gross and net site acreage, rounding and deductions depend on the local rule.

**Drill down.** Run the calculation in reverse in a meeting: 80 desired units ÷ 40 DU/acre needs at least two qualifying acres before considering the envelope. If a constraint removes half an acre, recalculate using the right denominator rather than “rounding up” the parcel.

**Primary checks:** City or county planning and permit authority. See the verification desk.

**Quiz — answer before reading the key**

### B3. Find the constraint that binds

Floor area ratio (FAR) is counted floor area divided by lot area. Setbacks reserve space from lot edges; lot coverage limits footprint; height limits vertical extent. Minimum lot size asks whether the qualifying lot area meets a local minimum; it is not necessarily a per-unit density rule. Together they shape the buildable envelope.

**Why it matters.** The largest FAR on paper is not always usable. An architect must satisfy overlapping rules simultaneously, including how the local code counts floors, parking and open space.

**Illustrative worked example.** On a 10,000-square-foot lot, FAR 2.0 permits up to 20,000 counted square feet. A 50% coverage limit allows a 5,000-square-foot footprint. If only three such floors fit, the gross stack is 15,000 before any further deductions.

**At the product desk.** Show WAZA’s native code, maximum FAR, height, density and minimum lot size, with missing fields visible. Do not turn one published maximum into an as-of-right promise.

**Meeting mistake.** “FAR 2 means two storeys.” A one-storey building covering all of a lot and a two-storey building covering half each have FAR 1.0 if all floor area counts.

**Drill down.** A setback is a geometric cut; coverage is an area limit. One can bind even if the other has slack. Check the official code’s definitions and exceptions and request a test fit. Do not assume a height in feet always converts to the same floor count.

**Primary checks:** City or county planning and permit authority. See the verification desk.

**Quiz — answer before reading the key**

1. Recall: Explain the building envelope in one sentence.
2. Apply: On the example lot, which is the tighter cap under the three-floor assumption?
3. Investor, 30 seconds: Why show more than one zoning field?

**Answer key.** Recall: Floor area ratio (FAR) is counted floor area divided by lot area. Setbacks reserve space from lot edges; lot coverage limits footprint; height limits vertical extent. Apply: 15,000 square feet from the footprint and floors. Use the constraint that binds, not the largest published number. Investor example: “A density limit counts homes and FAR counts floor area. **Add to your glossary:**

- FAR: Floor area ratio; counted floor area divided by qualifying lot area.
- Setback: Required separation from a lot line or other feature.
- Lot coverage: Share of lot area covered by the counted footprint.
- Height limit: Maximum height measured from a locally defined reference point.

### B4. Read the permission, not the color

By-right use can proceed under applicable objective rules without a discretionary use decision; it still needs required permits. Conditional use needs additional approval against criteria, often discretionary. A discretionary decision involves judgment by the authorized official or body.

**Why it matters.** The approval route changes carrying cost, timing and the probability of closing. A permitted use is not the same as an issued permit for a particular plan.

**Illustrative worked example.** Two 40-unit proposals have the same rents. If one needs six extra months of review at an illustrative $10,000 monthly carrying cost, that is $60,000 before considering denial risk.

**At the product desk.** In the atlas P means permitted, C conditional, X not permitted for the specified use. Keep this separate from the four broad map classes; commercial/mixed-use is not a blanket multifamily permission.

**Meeting mistake.** “C means approved with standard conditions.” It means a conditional path to evaluate, not an approval already granted.

**Drill down.** Ask the planner for the applicable use table, footnotes, thresholds, decision maker, appeal periods and conditions. A rezoning is a change of the underlying rule or map, not the same as applying for a conditional use under the existing rule. [VERIFY] The local ordinance controls the procedure.

**Primary checks:** City or county planning and permit authority. See the verification desk.

**Quiz — answer before reading the key**

### B5. A second layer can change the answer

A regulatory overlay adds rules to the base zoning for a mapped area. A map overlay in software is simply another visual layer; not every displayed layer is a zoning rule. A legal nonconforming use is a lawfully established use that no longer conforms to current rules.

## Chapter C · Build the income statement

Start with a full building at market rent. Walk down to what is collected, then subtract what it costs to operate. Keep the period consistent.

### C1. Start at full rent, not collected cash

Gross potential rent (GPR) is the rent a fully occupied building would generate at the stated rent basis. In this market-rent convention, loss to lease is the gap between assumed market rent and lower contractual rent on signed leases.

**Why it matters.** A broker can show an attractive market rent while tenants are locked into older leases. A buyer does not collect the higher number simply by buying the property.

**Illustrative worked example.** Ten units at $1,000 a month have $120,000 annual market GPR. If eight leases pay $950 and the other two are at market, annual loss to lease is 8 × $50 × 12 = $4,800. Scheduled rent is $115,200 before collection losses.

**At the product desk.** Tie the rent basis, unit count, rent-roll date and period to the projection. River North’s 180 versus 200 units changes GPR even with identical rents; no project rent is supplied.

**Meeting mistake.** Subtracting loss to lease again when the starting figure already uses contract rents. Ask “market or in-place?” before building the bridge.

**Drill down.** If a lease is above market, the sign may reverse. Recognizing a positive mark-to-market opportunity requires renewal assumptions, not just a subtraction. Check lease expirations and the cost of turning units.

**Quiz — answer before reading the key**

1. Recall: Explain gross potential rent in one sentence.
2. Apply: A River North model starts with contracted rents. Should it also subtract the entire market-to-contract gap?

**Add to your glossary:**

- GPR: Gross potential rent; full-occupancy rent at the specified rent basis.
- Loss to lease: Difference between market potential and lower contractual rents, not vacancy.

### C2. Bridge potential rent to effective income

Effective gross income (EGI) is expected operating revenue after rent losses, plus collectible other income. Vacancy is unoccupied time; concessions are discounts such as a free month. Bad debt is billed rent not collected.

**Why it matters.** A building can be physically occupied and still miss its revenue target. A free month, unpaid rent and an empty unit all reduce cash, for different reasons.

**Illustrative worked example.** Start with $120,000 potential rent. Subtract $4,800 loss to lease, $6,000 vacancy and $1,200 concessions. Add $3,000 parking income: EGI = $111,000. This example assumes no other losses.

**At the product desk.** A model comparison should show which losses and other-income lines are included, not merely compare two totals with different labels or periods.

**Meeting mistake.** Treating a security deposit as recurring other income, or deducting free rent both in effective lease rents and again as a concession.

**Drill down.** Physical vacancy counts empty homes; economic vacancy measures revenue shortfall under the stated convention. Ask whether a combined loss assumption already includes collection losses and concessions. Parking, laundry and storage need collection evidence too.

**Quiz — answer before reading the key**

1. Recall: Explain effective gross income in one sentence.
2. Apply: The rent assumption is already net of a free month. What do you do with a second concession line?
3. Investor, 30 seconds: Why can a full building have disappointing collections?

**Answer key.** Recall: Effective gross income (EGI) is expected operating revenue after rent losses, plus collectible other income. Make each reduction once, on the same period and rent basis. Investor example: “Occupancy tells me whether homes are leased. I still need the contractual rents, incentives and collections to know how much income those leases deliver.”

**Add to your glossary:**

- EGI: Effective gross income; revenue after rent losses plus collectible other operating income.
- Vacancy: Unoccupied capacity or associated rent loss; distinguish physical and economic measures.
- Concession: A rent incentive, such as a free month.

### C3. Underwrite the cost of staying open

Operating expenses are recurring costs of running the property, such as property taxes, insurance, repairs, utilities and management fees. An expense ratio here is operating expenses divided by effective gross income.

**Why it matters.** Expenses do not all fall when vacancy rises. Insurance renewals, assessed taxes and payroll can change independently of rent.

**Illustrative worked example.** At $111,000 effective gross income and $44,400 operating expense, the expense ratio is 40%. An illustrative management fee of 4% of that income is $4,440. If it is in the $44,400, do not add it again.

**At the product desk.** Keep assumptions separate from tax bills, insurance quotes and management contracts. County assessed value alone is not the next owner’s property tax expense.

**Meeting mistake.** Using an expense ratio from another property as though it were a complete, current operating budget.

**Drill down.** Read the fee base in the management agreement: collected revenue and scheduled revenue can differ. For taxes, verify the parcel and bill with the county; for insurance use coverage, deductibles and renewal terms. Separate recurring repairs from large capital replacements.

**Primary checks:** County assessor, treasurer and data custodian. See the verification desk.

**Quiz — answer before reading the key**

### C4. Stop before financing

Net operating income (NOI) is effective gross income minus property operating expenses for the same period. It measures property operations before debt service, income tax, depreciation and major capital spending.

**Why it matters.** NOI feeds valuation and loan sizing. Two documents can use the same label while one describes today’s operations and the other a future stabilized year.

**Illustrative worked example.** $111,000 effective gross income − $44,400 expenses = $66,600 annual NOI. In this before-reserves convention, a $5,000 replacement reserve leaves $61,600 before debt; it does not change the stated $66,600 NOI.

**At the product desk.** River North’s summary says $2.6 million NOI and the model says $2.5 million. The $100,000 difference is a flag, not evidence either author is wrong. Ask for periods, occupancy and reserve treatment.

**Meeting mistake.** Calling NOI “cash to equity.” Loan payments, capital work and reserves can still consume it.

**Drill down.** Replacement reserves set money aside for future major work. Lenders may deduct a reserve in their underwritten net cash flow. Reconcile the lender’s definition to the sponsor’s before calculating coverage or applying a cap rate. Stabilized means a specified sustainable operating state, not automatically year one.

**Quiz — answer before reading the key**

**Add to your glossary:**

## Chapter D · Read the returns and debt

Every ratio answers a particular question. Start with its numerator, name its denominator and say whose risk it measures.

### D1. Translate income into a price

A capitalization rate (cap rate) is annual net operating income (NOI) divided by property value or price on a consistent income basis. Reversing the equation gives an indicated value: NOI divided by a market-supported cap rate.

**Why it matters.** A buyer is purchasing an income stream. The cap rate relates that income to a price before financing; it is not a mortgage rate or the investor’s total return.

**Illustrative worked example.** $100,000 annual NOI ÷ $2 million price = 5%. At the same income, a 6% market cap indicates about $1.67 million in value. A higher required yield implies a lower price, all else equal.

**At the product desk.** River North’s $50 million model figure is project cost. No market value or purchase price for the completed property is supplied. Do not call $2.5 million ÷ $50 million a market cap rate.

**Meeting mistake.** Picking a cap rate because it makes the deal work. Ask for relevant sales, timing, asset quality and the income convention behind the comparables.

**Drill down.** Holding a hypothetical 5% cap rate fixed, a $100,000 change in annual NOI moves indicated value by $2 million. This sensitivity is not an appraisal. A trailing cap rate and a forward cap rate have different numerators; label which you use.

**Quiz — answer before reading the key**

1. Recall: Explain capitalization rate in one sentence.

**Answer key.** Recall: A capitalization rate (cap rate) is annual net operating income (NOI) divided by property value or price on a consistent income basis. **Add to your glossary:**

- Cap rate: Capitalization rate; annual NOI divided by price or value.
- Indicated value: NOI divided by an assumed market cap rate; an estimate, not a certified appraisal.

### D2. Compare the yield you build with the yield you buy

Development yield on cost (YOC) is stabilized annual net operating income divided by total development cost. Development spread is that yield minus a comparable market capitalization rate, using compatible income conventions.

**Illustrative worked example.** $600,000 stabilized NOI ÷ $10 million total cost = 6%. Against a hypothetical 5% market cap rate, the spread is 1 percentage point, or 100 basis points. It is not a 1% cash distribution.

**At the product desk.** If each River North NOI is stabilized annual income and each cost is all-in, the summary implies $2.6m ÷ $47.3m ≈ 5.50%; the model implies $2.5m ÷ $50m = 5.00%. The implied yields differ by about 50 basis points. Market spread is unknown.

**Meeting mistake.** Pairing the higher NOI from one document with the lower or newer cost from another without a reconciled scope.

**Drill down.** A spread can disappear through a cost overrun, slower lease-up or a higher market exit cap rate. Test each input separately, then together. The implied River North yields compare packages; they do not prove which package is later, approved or financeable.

**Add to your glossary:**

- Basis point: One hundredth of a percentage point; 100 basis points = 1 percentage point.

### D3. Ask whether income covers the payment

Debt service coverage ratio (DSCR) divides underwritten annual income available for debt service by annual debt service. Debt service is required interest plus scheduled principal. Use the lender’s income definition.

**Why it matters.** A lender wants a cushion against lower rents or higher expenses. If the numerator falls or payments rise, coverage shrinks even when the original loan balance is unchanged.

**Illustrative worked example.** $300,000 annual income ÷ $240,000 annual debt service = 1.25×. A 10% income drop gives $270,000 ÷ $240,000 = 1.125×. At 1.0× all of that income is needed for scheduled debt payments.

**At the product desk.** The River North figures contain no debt service. As a sensitivity only, $2.5m of eligible annual income at an assumed 1.25× target supports up to $2m annual debt service, not $2m of principal.

**Meeting mistake.** Testing interest-only coverage for a loan that later amortizes. An interest-only period pays no scheduled principal; an amortizing payment includes principal as well as interest.

**Drill down.** For a hypothetical $1 million interest-only balance at 6% for a full year, interest is $60,000. When principal repayment begins, use the actual payment schedule. To convert maximum annual payment into principal, you need rate, amortization and any interest-only period. [VERIFY] Read the actual lender term sheet, not an assumed agency minimum.

**Primary checks:** Fannie Mae · Multifamily Selling and Servicing Guide; Freddie Mac · Multifamily. See the verification desk.

**Quiz — answer before reading the key**

3. Investor, 30 seconds: Why not size a loan from an attractive NOI alone?

**Answer key.** Recall: Debt service coverage ratio (DSCR) divides underwritten annual income available for debt service by annual debt service. **Add to your glossary:**

- DSCR: Debt service coverage ratio; income available for debt divided by required debt service for the same period.
- Interest-only: Scheduled payments cover interest but do not amortize principal during the stated period.
- Amortizing: Payments repay scheduled principal as well as interest.

### D4. Test the loan against three different bases

**Why it matters.** A lender can apply multiple constraints. A strong appraisal does not fix inadequate cash flow, and a large construction budget does not guarantee a large loan.

**Illustrative worked example.** A $6m loan on $10m cost and $12m value has 60% LTC and 50% LTV. With $600,000 NOI, debt yield is 10%. An interest-rate change can alter coverage without changing that debt yield.

**At the product desk.** Show a metric only with its matching inputs. River North has cost but no approved loan amount or appraisal in the brief. Missing LTV, LTC and debt yield should not display as zero.

**Meeting mistake.** Treating total budget, lender-eligible cost and appraised value as interchangeable.

**Drill down.** For an illustration, a 60% LTC limit on $10m supports $6m; a 50% LTV limit on $11m supports $5.5m. If both apply, the smaller $5.5m ceiling binds before any coverage or debt-yield limit. Match as-is or stabilized value to the term sheet.

**Quiz — answer before reading the key**

**Add to your glossary:**

### D5. Separate how much from how fast

Equity multiple is total cash returned to equity, including returned capital, divided by equity contributed. Internal rate of return (IRR) is the discount rate at which dated net cash flows have zero net present value.

**Why it matters.** A multiple ignores time. IRR is sensitive to timing, including early distributions and a projected sale. Levered returns include borrowing, loan payments and repayment in the equity cash flows; unlevered returns measure the asset before financing.

**Illustrative worked example.** Invest $100 now and receive $150 once. The multiple is 1.5× whether the exit is in one year or three. Annual IRR is 50% in one year, but about 14.47% over three years since 1.1447³ ≈ 1.5.

**At the product desk.** A unit count, cost and one NOI cannot determine River North’s IRR. Request dates and amounts of contributions, operating cash flows, sale proceeds, costs and debt repayment.

**Meeting mistake.** Calling $150 a $150 profit after investing $100, or labeling a levered equity return as an unlevered building return.

**Drill down.** Dates matter for irregularly spaced flows. With repeated changes of sign, an IRR may not be unique or meaningful. Check the actual cash-flow schedule, multiple and downside case rather than ranking every deal on one headline IRR.

**Quiz — answer before reading the key**

**Add to your glossary:**

- IRR: Internal rate of return; discount rate making net present value zero for the cash-flow schedule.
- Equity multiple: Total cash returned divided by total equity contributed, without time weighting.
- Levered return: Equity return after financing flows.
- Unlevered return: Asset return without financing flows.

### E3. Time has a price before opening

Financing costs include loan fees and interest during development. A developer fee compensates the developer for work and risk under the deal terms. A fee can be a project use even if payment is deferred.

### F3. Read a rent roll one unit at a time

### F4. Resolve a discrepancy without erasing it

A documented value reports what a source says. A correction fixes an identifiable error; an estimate is an assumption where evidence is incomplete. An override is a separate human assertion with attribution, not an edit to the original evidence.

**Why it matters.** A lender may need to reconstruct a decision as it was made. If a source, correction and judgment call all look identical, the history cannot be trusted.

**Illustrative worked example.** Suppose a signed schedule reads 180 but an extraction shows 108. A reviewer can cite the page and correct the extraction. If a planner instead expects 190 next year, that is an estimate and needs a different label.

**At the product desk.** Preserve River North’s 180/200, $47.3m/$50m and $2.6m/$2.5m pairs. Identify scope and period; record who overrode what and when. Leave unresolved items unresolved. An append-only event log keeps prior actions.

**Meeting mistake.** Confusing permission to change a working number with authority to certify it. A Member’s estimate is not a city approval.

**Drill down.** A useful reconciliation memo names each source and definition, quantifies the difference, lists evidence still needed and states a working assumption with owner and date. If a later document resolves it, add a new event. Do not silently erase the old decision or disclose licensed data in a broadly readable log.

**Quiz — answer before reading the key**

1. Recall: Explain evidence-preserving overrides in one sentence.

**Answer key.** Recall: A documented value reports what a source says. A correction fixes an identifiable error; an estimate is an assumption where evidence is incomplete. An opinion does not become a documented correction by being entered into a tracker. Investor example: “I would give the working assumption an owner, date and estimate label. **Add to your glossary:**

- Override: Attributed working value layered over a preserved source value.
- Correction: A repair supported by identifiable evidence.
- Estimate: An explicit assumption, not a documented fact.
- Append-only log: History in which later events are added rather than editing or deleting previous events.

## Copy-paste glossary

- **Actuals:** Observed results for an identified period.
- **Agency debt:** Common market shorthand here for enterprise multifamily mortgage financing through participating lenders.
- **Amendment:** Document changing specified terms of an earlier agreement.
- **Amortizing:** Payments repay scheduled principal as well as interest.
- **Append-only log:** History in which later events are added rather than editing or deleting previous events.
- **Arrears:** Amounts owed but not yet paid.
- **Assessed value:** A value determined for property taxation, not necessarily the deal price or an appraisal for a loan.
- **Basis:** Tax starting value used in calculating gain or loss, subject to adjustments.
- **Basis point:** One hundredth of a percentage point; 100 basis points = 1 percentage point.
- **By right:** Allowed under applicable standards without a discretionary use decision; permits may still be required.
- **Cap rate:** Capitalization rate; annual NOI divided by price or value.
- **Cash-on-cash:** Period cash return divided by the stated cash equity base.
- **CDFI:** Community Development Financial Institution; a certified mission-driven financial institution, distinct from the Treasury fund that certifies it.
- **Census tract:** A statistical area; neither a parcel nor a land-use permission.
- **Change order:** Documented change to contract scope, price or time.
- **Comprehensive plan:** Long-range adopted policy, distinct from the development regulations used for a permit.
- **Concession:** A rent incentive, such as a free month.
- **Conditional use:** A use requiring additional review against specified criteria.
- **Contingency:** Budget allowance for uncertain costs on a specified base.
- **Correction:** A repair supported by identifiable evidence.
- **Crosswalk:** A correspondence between different geographic definitions, not a redesignation.
- **DDA:** Difficult Development Area; a cost relative to income LIHTC designation.
- **Debt yield:** Underwritten annual NOI divided by loan balance.
- **Deferral:** Postponing recognition of a gain; not the same as excluding it.
- **Density ceiling:** The maximum unit count implied by a density rule before other constraints.
- **Developer fee:** Compensation for development services; verify payment and deferral terms.
- **Development spread:** Yield on cost less a supported comparable market cap rate.
- **Diligence period:** Contractual investigation window subject to the actual agreement.
- **Distribution:** Cash paid to investors; distinguish operating, refinance and sale proceeds.
- **Draw:** An advance against an available loan or funding commitment.
- **DSCR:** Debt service coverage ratio; income available for debt divided by required debt service for the same period.
- **DU/acre:** Dwelling units per acre; state which acreage counts.
- **Earnest money:** Deposit governed by the contract’s refund, application and forfeiture provisions.
- **EGI:** Effective gross income; revenue after rent losses plus collectible other operating income.
- **Entitlement:** A land-use approval establishing specified development rights, often subject to conditions.
- **Equity multiple:** Total cash returned divided by total equity contributed, without time weighting.
- **Estimate:** An explicit assumption, not a documented fact.
- **Expense ratio:** Operating expenses divided by the specified revenue measure, here EGI.
- **FAR:** Floor area ratio; counted floor area divided by qualifying lot area.
- **FHFA:** Federal Housing Finance Agency; regulator of Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac.
- **FMR:** Fair Market Rent; gross rent benchmark by place and bedroom count.
- **Funding gap:** Uses in excess of identified sources; distinguish missing data from a measured shortfall.
- **GMA:** Growth Management Act; Washington’s statutory planning framework.
- **GPR:** Gross potential rent; full-occupancy rent at the specified rent basis.
- **Gross building area:** Floor area including common and service space under a stated standard.
- **Hard costs:** Physical construction and site work.
- **Height limit:** Maximum height measured from a locally defined reference point.
- **HUD:** U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development; federal housing agency.
- **Indicated value:** NOI divided by an assumed market cap rate; an estimate, not a certified appraisal.
- **Interest reserve:** Budgeted funding for interest during a specified period.
- **Interest-only:** Scheduled payments cover interest but do not amortize principal during the stated period.
- **IRR:** Internal rate of return; discount rate making net present value zero for the cash-flow schedule.
- **IRS:** Internal Revenue Service; administers federal tax law.
- **Lease-up:** Period of moving from initial availability toward stabilized occupancy.
- **Levered return:** Equity return after financing flows.
- **LIHTC:** Low-Income Housing Tax Credit; a federal affordable rental housing credit generally allocated through state housing agencies.
- **Loss to lease:** Difference between market potential and lower contractual rents, not vacancy.
- **Lot coverage:** Share of lot area covered by the counted footprint.
- **LTC:** Loan-to-cost; loan divided by lender-eligible project cost.
- **LTV:** Loan-to-value; loan divided by matching property value.
- **Management fee:** Payment for managing operations; confirm the percentage or flat fee and its base.
- **Middle housing:** Housing forms such as duplexes and small multiplexes, between a detached house and a large apartment building.
- **Net rentable area:** Area used to calculate rent under the stated convention.
- **NMTC:** New Markets Tax Credit; allocation authority awarded to qualifying Community Development Entities.
- **NOI:** Net operating income; effective gross income less property operating expenses.
- **Nonconforming use:** Lawfully established use that no longer conforms; continuation rights depend on local law.
- **Operating expense:** Recurring cost to operate the asset; not principal repayment or construction cost.
- **Overlay:** An additional mapped layer; a regulatory overlay may impose rules on top of the base zone.
- **Override:** Attributed working value layered over a preserved source value.
- **OZ:** Opportunity Zone; a Treasury-certified geographic designation used in an investment tax regime.
- **Parcel:** A unit of land represented in a land or tax record; a map polygon is not a legal survey.
- **Preferred return:** Contractual priority in distributions under specified terms.
- **Pro forma:** Forward-looking financial projection under stated assumptions.
- **Promote:** Sponsor’s disproportionate participation after specified thresholds.
- **Provenance:** Source document, version and date behind a value.
- **QCT:** Qualified Census Tract; an income or poverty based LIHTC designation.
- **QOF:** Qualified Opportunity Fund; investment vehicle subject to qualifying asset rules.
- **QOZB:** Qualified Opportunity Zone Business; a business subject to separate qualifying requirements.
- **Rent roll:** Dated unit-level schedule of occupancy and lease terms.
- **Replacement reserve:** Funds set aside for future replacements; state where deducted.
- **Rezoning:** Changing a zoning classification or map through the applicable process.
- **Setback:** Required separation from a lot line or other feature.
- **Soft costs:** Nonconstruction project services such as design, permits and legal work.
- **Sources and uses:** Funding sources reconciled to every project use on the same scope and date.
- **Stabilized:** An assumed sustainable operating level after lease-up or disruption, not necessarily present performance.
- **Total development cost:** All project uses for a stated scope and period, not just construction.
- **Underwriting:** Testing evidence and assumptions to decide risk, terms and size of an investment or loan.
- **Unit mix:** Counts by unit type, often with areas and rents.
- **Unlevered return:** Asset return without financing flows.
- **Vacancy:** Unoccupied capacity or associated rent loss; distinguish physical and economic measures.
- **Vintage:** The reference period of a dataset or boundary definition.
- **Waterfall:** Ordered rules allocating available cash to investors and sponsor.
- **WAZA:** Washington State Zoning Atlas; generalized research snapshot, not a legal zoning determination.
- **YOC:** Yield on cost; stabilized annual NOI divided by total development cost.

- [HUD USER · Qualified Census Tracts and Difficult Development Areas](https://www.huduser.gov/portal/datasets/qct.html): Use the official designation lists for the applicable year and program.

- [U.S. Census Bureau · TIGER/Line geographic files](https://www.census.gov/geographies/mapping-files/time-series/geo/tiger-line-file.html): Use the 2010 census tract geography for the legacy designations. Check relationship files for any decade comparison.

- **County assessor, treasurer and data custodian**: Check the parcel identifier, assessment year, tax bill, metadata and license. For legal boundaries request title and survey evidence.

- **Executed project documents and provider agreements**: Read amendments, terms and effective dates. Confirm legal terms with counsel and financial assumptions with the responsible lender or underwriter.
