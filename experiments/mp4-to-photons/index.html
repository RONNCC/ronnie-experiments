Start here / 10 stops

Follow the signal.

New to media? Follow these ten stops, then try the engineering route. Already coding? Jump to worked examples, real timestamps and pipeline checks below.

01
First, the wrapper

It starts with a container.

An MP4 is a box of instructions, not a single stream of pictures.

Think of a shipping box with a packing list. The container holds compressed video and audio plus information about where they are and when to play them. It doesn't itself define how pictures or sounds are compressed.

Go deeper: what do the boxes do?

ftyp declares compatible file types; moov contains movie and track metadata; mdat contains media data. Track tables describe sample sizes, timing and locations. A “sample” here is a container unit, not necessarily one audio waveform sample. Files can arrange these boxes differently. [1]

02
Unpack

Separate the streams.

Demuxing finds the right pieces and sends them to the right decoder.

A demuxer reads the track tables and extracts compressed samples, along with their timestamps. The pieces may be interleaved in the file; video goes to a video decoder, audio to an audio decoder.

One container, two pathsSimplified model
MP4

Go deeper: timestamps

Decode timestamps order work for the decoder; presentation timestamps say when output should be shown. They can differ because some video pictures depend on later pictures. The container provides timing, not a guarantee that the hardware meets it. [1, 2]

03
Reconstruct

Video: predict, then correct.

A decoder rebuilds images from compressed clues.

H.264 often predicts a block from nearby pixels or previously decoded pictures, then encodes the difference, called a residual. The decoder reconstructs the prediction and adds the decoded correction. This is a teaching model, not the full codec. [2]

Color is often stored as luma (roughly brightness) and two chroma channels (color differences). In 4:2:0, each 2 × 2 group of luma samples shares one sample of each chroma channel. Fine color edges can soften even when brightness stays detailed. [3]

Go deeper: what else is in H.264?

Intra prediction can use neighbors in the same picture; inter prediction uses motion-compensated reference pictures. Transform, quantization and entropy coding reduce data further; decoding reverses these steps approximately. Compression can be lossy, so reconstructed pictures need not equal the original. [2]

04
Reconstruct

Audio becomes numbers in time.

AAC turns compressed audio into PCM samples.

The AAC decoder reconstructs PCM: a sequence of numbers representing signal amplitude at evenly spaced times. At 44,100 samples per second, each channel has 44,100 values per second. Those numbers are not sound yet. [4]

Keep the distinction: AAC is compressed audio; PCM is the decoded waveform representation. A file's audio sample rate can differ from the device's output rate, requiring resampling.
Go deeper: lossy audio

AAC uses transform-based coding and perceptual techniques to represent sound efficiently. It discards or approximates information; decoding produces an approximation, not the original recording. [4]

05
Arrange

Make a picture for the screen.

The GPU and compositor put the pieces together.

Decoded video may be in a Y′CbCr format (often casually called YUV). A conversion maps luma and color differences to red, green and blue. The image may be scaled to fit a window, then combined with subtitles, other windows and the desktop. Work can be shared between hardware blocks, GPU and display engine. [3, 5]

From decoded frame to desktopSimplified model
Y′ + Cb + Cr→Convert to RGB→Scale + layers→Display buffer

Actual ordering varies. Color conversion depends on matrix, range and transfer characteristics; it isn't one universal formula.

Go deeper: composition

A compositor decides what surfaces are visible and how they overlap. A display controller can sometimes place video on a hardware plane directly instead of first blending everything on the GPU. [5]

06
Keep time

24 frames, 60 refreshes.

A movie and a display don't necessarily share a rhythm.

A frame is a decoded picture. Refresh rate is how often a panel is updated. For exactly 24 fps on a fixed 60 Hz display, each picture lasts alternately 2 and 3 refreshes: a 2:3 pulldown. At 120 Hz, each can last exactly 5 refreshes. Uneven holds can cause judder. [5]

Watch the cadenceAnimated / simplified

The moving line illustrates scanout: a display controller reads out a frame progressively, usually row by row. This is slowed down dramatically and not a panel timing diagram.

Go deeper: other cadences

Many films are 23.976 rather than exactly 24 fps. Variable refresh, frame interpolation and different display modes can change the strategy. A buffer swap at the wrong time can show parts of two frames (tearing). [5]

07
Transport

Cross the display link.

The finished image travels as a timed electrical signal.

HDMI and DisplayPort connect devices to displays; eDP is a DisplayPort variant often used inside laptops. Links carry video along with control and auxiliary information; HDMI and DisplayPort use different framing and link mechanisms. The diagram is conceptual, not their wire format. [6, 7]

How much data?Labeled model / uncompressed

Example active pixels only: 1920 × 1080 × 60 refreshes/s × 24 bits per RGB pixel ≈ 2.99 Gbit/s. This is not the required link rate: blanking, encoding, protocol overhead, compression and chroma format can change it. Audio can also be carried over these links.

08
Light

Meet the subpixel.

Tiny colored components combine into the light you see.

An LCD uses a backlight and liquid-crystal cells to modulate light passing through color filters. An OLED uses light-emitting organic devices; each emissive element produces light when driven. Real subpixel layouts and OLED color architectures vary. [8, 9]

Subpixel zoomInteractive / conceptual RGB

As you move the control, the RGB intensities change. In a real panel the drive, color response and arrangement are more complicated; the glow is illustrative.

09
Sound

Numbers move air.

The other half of the journey is mechanical.

PCM reaches a DAC, which converts digital values to an analog electrical signal. An amplifier supplies power to a speaker. Current in its coil interacts with a magnet, moving a diaphragm; pressure waves travel through air to your eardrum. The cochlea converts mechanical motion into signals for the nervous system. [10, 11]

Speaker & sampling labInteractive / simplified
▰◀)))

Choose a model and press play. A quiet tone plays for exactly 2 seconds only after your click. Start with low volume. These are generated examples, not the MP4 audio.

A smooth 440 Hz reference at the audio device's sample rate.

Sampling below twice a tone's frequency can make it indistinguishable from a lower frequency: 3,600 Hz at 4,000 samples/s aliases to 400 Hz. The lab synthesizes that low-rate sequence and holds each sample, then plays it through your device. Lower bit depth rounds amplitude to fewer levels, adding quantization distortion. Real converters use filtering and may use dithering. [10, 12]

10
Together again

Make the lips match.

Separate paths have to meet at the right time.

Video and audio take different amounts of time to decode and travel through hardware. Players buffer data and schedule presentation against clocks and timestamps. Too much offset makes a voice arrive before or after the mouth movement. Devices and operating systems add their own latency. [1, 5]

And that's the loop. File bytes become reconstructed pictures and samples, then timed light and moving air. Your brain does the rest.
The engineering route / build and verify

From model output to playable media.

A model can emit waveform values or a stack of images. A player needs something stricter: known units, formats, timestamps and bounded delivery. This route connects the teaching demos above to that engineering work.

What runs here: native browser playback of the bundled MP4, generated WebAudio tones, and arithmetic/visual teaching models. This page does not implement an H.264/AAC codec, an AI model, a production stream server or a hardware latency probe. The commands below run on your own machine with FFmpeg installed.

1. Give every output a contract.

Before connecting a speech or video model to an encoder, make its implicit assumptions explicit. A tensor is a shaped array of numbers; its shape alone does not tell you their meaning.

Audio example
Float32 PCM; 48,000 sample frames/second; 2 channels; planar arrays [channel][sample]; finite values with an intended range of -1 to +1; explicit channel order; sample-frame index 0 anchored to a known start time.

Video example
RGB frames; 128 × 72; declared numeric range and color interpretation; frame index i presented at i/24 seconds for this constant-rate example. Preserve explicit per-frame timestamps instead if the source is variable-rate.

A model might instead emit mono 24 kHz audio, normalized float RGB, uint8 pixels, mel-spectrograms or codec tokens. A spectrogram or token sequence is not PCM: it needs the appropriate model-specific decoder/vocoder. Do not relabel 24 kHz samples as 48 kHz to “convert” them: that doubles speed and pitch. Resample the signal. Do not resample the two channels as one interleaved time series.

Boundary checks before encoding
  • Check shape, channel count, sample rate, duration, NaN/Infinity and peaks. Decide whether out-of-range floats are clipped, normalized or rejected. Loudness normalization and peak limiting solve different problems.
  • Record model/version, seed when meaningful, source asset hashes and conversion settings. Keep consent and provenance alongside generated assets, not inside a filename alone.
  • For video, decide dimensions, alpha handling, primaries, transfer function, matrix and range. RGB to Y′CbCr conversion changes representation; adding metadata alone does not perform that conversion. Do not tag an unknown image as HDR or Rec.709 and assume it is correct.

2. PCM: storage, sampling and the actual tone code.

A sample frame is one instant across all channels. Two seconds of stereo Float32 at 48 kHz is 96,000 sample frames, 192,000 values and 768,000 bytes before object overhead. A two-second mono tone here allocates 2 × audioContext.sampleRate values, so its exact size depends on the output context.

bytes = seconds × sampleRate × channels × bytesPerValue
x[n] = A · sin(2π f n / sampleRate)

The live tone generator writes PCM into an AudioBuffer, connects an AudioBufferSourceNode to the destination and starts it only after a click. It uses amplitude 0.045 and 20 ms attack/release ramps to reduce boundary clicks. This is not a calibrated loudness level or a safety guarantee: keep device volume low. [10]

The aliasing demo deliberately samples a 3,600 Hz sine at 4,000 Hz. At those sample instants it is indistinguishable from a signed 400 Hz sine: |3600 - 4000| = 400 Hz. Holding those samples at the device rate also creates spectral images, so the result is not a pure 400 Hz oscillator. Float32 describes amplitude precision, not temporal resolution: more bits do not prevent aliasing. A proper downsampler low-pass filters before reducing the rate; interpolation after aliasing cannot recover what was lost. [12, 13]

The 3-bit demo rounds the full-scale sine to eight levels before applying its quiet gain and envelope. Thus it demonstrates quantization distortion, not a bit-exact 3-bit output device. Its adjacent quantizer levels are 2/7 apart. It applies no dither. Dither is noise added before quantization to reduce signal-correlated error, not a way to restore lost information. [17]

3. Encode a known asset, then interrogate it.

This command creates a new, comparable test asset, not a promise of byte-identical reproduction of the bundled file. Encoder versions and defaults affect bytes, packet ordering and metadata. The lavfi inputs generate test signals; no model or user upload is involved. [14, 15]

ffmpeg -f lavfi -i testsrc2=size=128x72:rate=24:duration=1 \
  -f lavfi -i sine=frequency=440:sample_rate=44100:duration=1 \
  -map 0:v:0 -map 1:a:0 -c:v libx264 -pix_fmt yuv420p \
  -c:a aac -b:a 96k -movflags +faststart -t 1 example.mp4

ffprobe -v error -show_streams -show_format -of json example.mp4
ffprobe -v error -select_streams v:0 -show_packets \
  -show_entries packet=pts,dts,pts_time,dts_time,duration_time,flags \
  -of csv=p=0 example.mp4
ffmpeg -v error -i example.mp4 -f null -

-map makes track selection explicit. H.264 compresses video; AAC compresses audio; MP4 muxes the tracks. +faststart moves movie metadata toward the beginning of a conventional file to help progressive playback. It is not adaptive streaming or fragmented MP4. The last command performs a decode pass; silence there means no errors reported at that log level, not proof of perceptual quality.

Inspect packet side data, edit lists and decoded output when trimming or concatenating; do not discard a negative timestamp just because it looks wrong.

4. Follow one compressed sample through MP4.

For this conventional, non-fragmented sample, the track's sample table connects playback time to bytes. These are different questions, not interchangeable tables. The linked sample-table walkthrough provides a concrete example. [18]

TableQuestion it answers
sttsWhat decode-time delta does each run of samples use? Accumulate deltas to obtain decode times.
cttsWhat composition offset shifts decode time to presentation time? Offset handling depends on box version.
stscHow are samples grouped into chunks, and which sample description applies?
stszHow many bytes are in a sample?
stco / co64Where does each chunk begin in the file, with 32- or 64-bit offsets?
stssWhich samples are sync samples for random access? Dependency/preroll details can still matter.
stsdWhich sample entry and codec configuration describe the compressed samples?

To find sample bytes: identify its chunk via stsc, take that chunk's file offset, then add the sizes of preceding samples inside the chunk. Timing is a separate lookup. An edit list can map track media time onto the movie timeline. For fragmented MP4 the relevant layout changes: initialization metadata and moof/traf/tfdt/trun fragments supply sample timing and locations. A browser's Media Source pipeline has its own segment requirements. [1, 16]

5. Decode order is not display order.

A decoder may need a future reference picture before displaying an earlier B-picture. DTS schedules decoding; PTS schedules presentation. FFprobe's packet view of the actual sample starts as follows, in decode order:

6. Sync is a clock problem and a queue problem.

Exactly 24 fps on 60 Hz requires alternating 2- and 3-refresh holds; this is uneven spacing even when no frame is “dropped.” Do not silently substitute 24 for 24000/1001 (about 23.976). At 100 parts per million, two otherwise independent clocks separate by 60 ms over ten minutes. Real players choose a clock and correct drift; starting two timers together is insufficient.

Keep at least three quantities separate: media timestamps (where output belongs), monotonic scheduling time (when work should run), and wall-clock time (a calendar reading). The A/V slider above illustrates a constant offset only. Drift is an offset that changes over time; network jitter is variable delivery delay.

For live generated media, log a latency budget: model startup + chunk generation + buffering + encode + transfer + decode + output. Some stages overlap, so an end-to-end trace is more useful than blindly summing averages. A 40 ms queue at 48 kHz contains 1,920 sample frames per channel. Smaller queues lower delay but leave less room for jitter and can underrun.

  • Backpressure: bound queues in bytes and media duration. A fast producer must slow, pause or use an explicit drop policy when the consumer cannot keep up. Unbounded queues turn a live stream into a delayed recording.
  • Continuity: derive chunk timestamps from cumulative sample counts, not request completion times. Preserve fractional video rates as rational numbers; repeated rounding can accumulate error.
  • Chunk seams: preserve resampler/filter state, avoid waveform discontinuities and test final partial chunks. AAC priming/padding on independently encoded chunks needs special care; concatenating arbitrary MP4 bytes is not a muxing strategy.
  • Browser limits: use the audio clock for scheduled sound, not a setInterval deadline. An AudioWorklet can support ongoing real-time audio processing; the two-second buffer demo here is not such a stream. [19] Video callbacks and audio timestamps can help diagnostics but do not directly measure photons or sound leaving hardware.

7. A small acceptance test, not “it plays on my laptop.”

  1. Structural: inspect codecs, channels, dimensions, duration, timebases, color fields and intended browser compatibility. Check more than a filename extension.
  2. Temporal: decode the full file; inspect packet and decoded-frame timing, first/last samples and A/V end times. Preserve VFR timestamps unless an intentional CFR conversion duplicates/drops frames. [14, 15]
  3. Perceptual: listen for clicks, clipping and gaps at seams; use a visible flash and matching click to test sync. Use real capture equipment for physical output latency claims.
  4. Operational: test slow consumers, stalls, cancellations, corrupt/truncated chunks and recovery. Track queue duration, underruns, late/dropped frames and time to first playable output.
