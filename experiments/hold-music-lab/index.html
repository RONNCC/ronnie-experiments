LAB 00 / START HERE, NO BACKGROUND NEEDED

The Reddit answer, graded

The question comes from r/explainlikeimfive: Why does most hold music sound so terrible over the phone? The top comment, by ChefGorton, says the standards were created when wires carried very little data, the data is compressed and optimized for the range of regular human speech, and music cannot be well represented that way. That is essentially correct. This lab turns that one paragraph into the four concrete mechanisms underneath it, so you can see exactly what gets thrown away and why.

What it gets right

Built for speech, not music

Phone standards were designed in the era of analog copper pairs and early digital trunks. The channel was engineered to carry the frequencies that make words intelligible, and nothing else. Music needs far more. Labs 1 and 2 show the exact numbers.

What it gets right

Compression is real, and brutal

A phone call uses a few thousand bits per second where a music stream uses hundreds of thousands. The codec is not a generic compressor. It is a speech model that assumes a human vocal tract. Lab 3 opens that model up.

What it leaves out

It is not just one filter

Hold music suffers at least four separate hits: a band limit, a low sample rate, companding quantization, and a speech codec, plus network jitter and loss on top. Each lab isolates one hit so you can hear and see it alone.

What it leaves out

Why it persists

Modern codecs can carry full band music easily. Hold music still sounds bad because the call usually crosses into the old telephone network, where every device must agree on the lowest common denominator. Lab 5 explains the trap.

The 30 second version

Imagine a phone call as a narrow tunnel that only lets through the middle pitches of a human voice. Music has deep bass, sparkling highs, and many instruments at once, so most of it hits the tunnel walls and is lost. What survives is then chopped into 8,000 tiny snapshots per second and squeezed into a speech shaped box. Words survive that journey. Music does not.

Predict first. If you could only keep one slice of a song and still recognize the words of a singer, which slice would you keep?

LAB 01 / FREQUENCY

The tunnel: 300 to 3400 Hz

Every classic telephone channel passes roughly 300 Hz to 3400 Hz and attenuates everything outside that band. The band was chosen because the formants of speech, the resonant frequencies that distinguish vowels, sit mostly inside it. A sung vowel keeps its identity there. A piano chord does not. Drag the tunnel walls and watch what survives.

Predict first. A piano note at 110 Hz with harmonics at 220, 330, 440 Hz and above enters the tunnel. What happens to its pitch?

With the classic walls at 300 and 3400 Hz, most music energy is outside the tunnel. Speech formants sit inside it.

Why those exact numbers

The public switched telephone network, or PSTN, allocates each voice channel a 4 kHz slot. Guard bands at the edges keep neighboring channels from leaking into each other, leaving about 3.1 kHz of usable passband. Bell System and later ITU measurements showed that band limited speech in that range stays intelligible even though it sounds muffled. Intelligibility was the design goal, not fidelity.

Passband ≈ 300 Hz to 3400 Hz  ·  Channel slot = 4000 Hz

What music loses

  • Bass below 300 Hz: kick drum, bass guitar, piano fundamentals. The beat survives only as harmonics.
  • Air above 3400 Hz: cymbal shimmer, string bow texture, consonant crispness, reverb tails.
  • Stereo width: phone channels are mono. Panning and chorus collapse to the center.

The spectrum curves here are a teaching model shaped from typical long term average spectra, not a measurement of one recording. The passband edges are the real standard values.

LAB 02 / SAMPLING

Sampling: 8000 snapshots per second

A digital phone does not carry a continuous wave. It takes 8000 snapshots per second of the waveform and sends those numbers. The Nyquist rule says you can only reconstruct frequencies below half the snapshot rate. At 8000 snapshots, that ceiling is 4000 Hz, which is exactly why the tunnel in Lab 1 ends near 3400 Hz. Push the tone above the ceiling and watch it alias into a false lower pitch.

Predict first. You sample a 6000 Hz tone at 8000 snapshots per second. What pitch do you hear back?

Nyquist and aliasing Interactive · exact math

1200 Hz is below the 4000 Hz ceiling, so the samples reconstruct the tone correctly.

Nyquist ceiling = sample rate / 2  ·  Need fs > 2 × fmax

Why 8000, not 6800

To preserve 3400 Hz you need more than 6800 snapshots per second. Engineers picked 8000 because it divides cleanly into the 64 kHz digital trunk clocks of the era and leaves a 600 Hz guard band for the anti alias filter. That filter must remove everything above 4000 Hz before sampling, or those frequencies fold back as audible aliasing. Music has plenty of energy above 4000 Hz, so the filter deletes it permanently.

Anti alias filtering is why hold music sounds dull rather than buzzy. Aliasing would add false tones on top. The dullness is the filter doing its job.

LAB 03 / COMPRESSION

Squeezing bits: mu-law and speech codecs

After sampling, the phone must shrink each snapshot. The classic trick is mu-law companding: loud sounds get fewer quantization steps, quiet sounds get more, matching how ears and speech behave. Later codecs go further and model the vocal tract directly. Both tricks assume speech. Music, with its wide dynamics and dense harmonics, exposes every shortcut.

Predict first. Mu-law gives quiet sounds more precision and loud sounds less. What does that do to a quiet piano passage played under a loud chorus?

Mu-law companding curve Interactive · exact formula

Mu of 255 is the G.711 standard. The curve compresses loud amplitudes so 8 bits can cover a wide dynamic range.

y = sign(x) × ln(1 + μ|x|) / ln(1 + μ)  ·  μ = 255 for G.711

From 64 kbps down to 8 kbps

G.711 sends 8000 samples at 8 bits each, which is 64 kbps. That was already a compromise: 8 bit linear audio would clip or hiss badly. Mu-law makes 8 bits behave like roughly 12 to 13 bits for speech. Modern speech codecs go much further by sending model parameters instead of samples: a pitch period, a spectral envelope, and an excitation signal. That is why G.729 reaches 8 kbps and still sounds like a person, while music through the same codec turns to warbling mush.

Bitrates are the standard nominal values. Music streaming at 320 kbps is shown for scale. The gap is the whole story.

What the phone chain actually does here

  1. Band limit: high pass at 300 Hz and low pass at 3400 Hz remove bass and air.
  2. Companding: a mu-law style waveshaper adds the coarse quantization character of G.711.
  3. Mono collapse: the loop is summed to one channel, as on a real call.

The wideband mode keeps 50 to 7000 Hz, which is why HD Voice sounds noticeably more natural. It still is not full fidelity, and that gap is the point: each standard is a deliberate trade between bandwidth and quality.

This is a teaching simulation using standard filter types and the real cutoff frequencies. It is not a bit exact G.711 or G.729 encoder. The perceptual effect, thin and muffled music with intelligible voice, matches real calls.

LAB 05 / THE NETWORK

Why it never got fixed: the call path

Hold music rarely travels end to end on modern codecs. It is injected by a phone system, often transcoded into the telephone band, carried as tiny packets, and decoded by a handset that expects speech. Every hop can downgrade the audio. The animation below shows packets moving through that path. Add loss and jitter and watch the quality meter fall.

Call path packet animation Interactive · simplified timing model

Estimated call quality: Good Low loss and jitter. Speech and music both survive.

At 0% loss and low jitter, packets arrive in order and the jitter buffer hides small timing wobbles.

The hops, in order

  1. Source: hold music file on the phone system, often already low quality MP3.
  2. Encode: converted to G.711 or G.729 narrowband. This is where the damage happens.
  3. Packetize: 20 ms of audio per packet for VoIP, 160 samples at 8 kHz.
  4. Network: packets can arrive late, out of order, or not at all. A jitter buffer delays playback to smooth this out.
  5. Decode and play: the handset decodes speech and plays it through a small speaker with its own limits.

Why jitter hurts music more

Speech tolerates small gaps because the brain fills them in. Music exposes every gap as a stutter or a warble, because rhythm and harmony leave no room for guessing. Echo cancellers, built to remove the far end voice leaking back, can also misfire on sustained music and pump its volume.

The quality meter here is a teaching heuristic combining loss and jitter, not a standardized MOS score. Real measurements use models such as E-model or POLQA.

LAB 06 / THE FIX

The fix already exists, and it works

Wideband voice doubles the tunnel to 50 to 7000 Hz. Fullband codecs such as Opus and EVS carry the full 20 Hz to 20 kHz range and adapt their bitrate to the network. When both ends support them, calls sound dramatically more natural and music becomes recognizable. The problem is not technology. It is agreement: every device, carrier, and gateway on the path must support the better codec at the same time.

Predict first. Both phones support Opus fullband, but the call is routed through a legacy gateway that only supports G.711. What do you hear?

Passband expansion over time Interactive · standards history

G.711 in 1972: 300 to 3400 Hz at 8 kHz sampling. Everything since has been about widening this tunnel.

What wideband buys you

Doubling the top of the tunnel to 7000 Hz restores consonant crispness and the sense of a real room. Voices gain presence. Music gains recognizable timbre, though bass below 50 Hz is still gone. This is the HD Voice experience on modern mobile networks.

What fullband buys you

Fullband carries the whole audible range and adapts bitrate moment by moment. Opus can drop to speech only efficiency on a weak network and expand to music quality when bandwidth allows. That adaptivity is why it replaced older codecs in WebRTC and many VoIP systems.

Why hold music lags behind

Hold music is often a licensed loop played from a server that was built for the PSTN era. Even when the call itself is VoIP, the music source, the gateway, or the handset may force a downgrade. Fixing hold music means fixing the whole chain, not just one codec.

Glossary

Passband

The frequency range a channel passes without heavy attenuation. The phone passband is about 300 to 3400 Hz.

Formant

A resonant peak in the vocal tract spectrum. Vowel identity lives in the first three formants, mostly inside the phone band.

Nyquist rate

Twice the highest frequency to be preserved. Sampling at 8000 Hz preserves up to 4000 Hz.

Aliasing

False tones created when a frequency above the Nyquist ceiling folds back into the audible band during sampling.

Companding

Compressing dynamic range before quantization and expanding after, so quiet sounds get finer steps. Mu-law is the classic example.

Codec

An encoder and decoder pair that compresses audio. Speech codecs model the vocal tract. Music codecs preserve waveforms and spectra.

LPC

Linear predictive coding. The codec predicts each sample from previous ones and only sends the error plus model parameters.

Transcoding

Decoding one codec and re-encoding into another. Each pass loses quality. Gateways transcode between VoIP and PSTN.

Jitter buffer

A small delay buffer that smooths packet arrival timing so playback stays continuous, at the cost of added latency.

Narrowband vs wideband

Narrowband is the 300 to 3400 Hz phone band. Wideband extends to 7000 Hz. Fullband extends to 20 kHz.
