How blood makes a plug.
And why tests get tricky.

Blood can form a plug to stop a cut from bleeding. Eliquis slows this process. A different thing, an immune protein called lupus anticoagulant, can make a lab test misleading. Start with the stories; no biology background needed.

First, meet the cast.

Imagine a leak in a pipe. Blood makes a temporary plug, then strengthens it with a net. These words are the characters in that story; you can come back to them at any time.

Clot — a plug made by blood to stop a leak. Inside a blood vessel, a clot can also block the flow.

Plasma — the liquid part of blood, like the broth carrying ingredients in soup.

Platelet — a tiny piece of a blood cell that gathers at a cut and helps make the first plug.

Factor — a protein that takes part in clotting; each numbered factor is a different relay runner.

Enzyme — a protein that helps a chemical reaction happen, like a worker operating a machine.

Fibrin — sticky protein threads that weave a mesh to reinforce a clot, like netting over a patch.

Phospholipid — a fatty building block of cell surfaces; it provides a workbench where clotting proteins can assemble.

Antibody — an immune-system protein that recognizes a target, like a lock fitting a key.

Anticoagulant — a medicine that slows clotting, often called a blood thinner. It does not literally dilute blood.

Assay — a laboratory test. A clotting assay measures what happens to a blood sample in a tube.

Ratio — one number divided by another; for example, 60 seconds ÷ 30 seconds is a ratio of 2.

Thrombin — a clotting protein that sends the instruction to turn fibrinogen into fibrin threads.

Fibrinogen — a protein dissolved in plasma that thrombin changes into fibrin, the net’s threads.

Antithrombin — a natural protein that acts as a brake on clotting; some medicines boost it.

A cascade is a chain reaction: one step turns on the next, like dominoes. “Activated” means a factor has been switched on. The small “a” in Xa means activated factor X. Despite its name, lupus anticoagulant is an antibody pattern, not an anticoagulant medicine.

01 / THE MOLECULE MECHANISM OF ACTION

A small molecule.
A pivotal target.

A clotting cascade is a relay race: one factor switches on the next. At the finish line is a clot, a mesh that plugs a cut. We need clots to stop bleeding, but an unwanted clot can block a blood vessel. Eliquis (also called apixaban) slows the relay by blocking one key runner, factor Xa.

THE CENTRAL IDEA

Less factor Xa activity.
Less thrombin generation.

01 / USE

Where it fits

Eliquis helps prevent stroke and traveling clots in some people with atrial fibrillation (an irregular heartbeat not caused by certain valve problems). It also treats and helps prevent deep vein thrombosis (DVT, a clot in a deep vein) and pulmonary embolism (PE, a clot in the lungs); it can help prevent DVT after hip or knee replacement.

For treatment of a new DVT or PE (together called venous thromboembolism, or VTE), the usual adult starting dose is 10 mg twice daily for 7 days, then 5 mg twice daily. Extended prevention has a separate regimen.

02 / DOSE

When less is more

For atrial fibrillation (AF) without the specified valve problems, the label reduces the dose from 5 mg to 2.5 mg twice daily when at least two are present: age ≥80 years, weight ≤60 kg, or a kidney-function blood marker called serum creatinine ≥1.5 mg/dL. This rule is not the VTE treatment dosing rule.

03 / SAFETY

Bleeding & procedures

Bleeding is the principal risk. For life-threatening or uncontrolled bleeding, andexanet alfa is a medicine that can reverse the effect; a concentrated mixture of clotting proteins called 4-factor PCC may also be considered under local protocols.

Label guidance: hold at least 24 h for low-risk procedures, and 48 h for moderate/high bleeding risk. The treating team adjusts the plan for kidney function, the procedure and clot risk. Do not hold or restart the medicine on your own.

02 / THE PARADOX LUPUS ANTICOAGULANT

Longer in the tube.
Clots in the body.

Lupus anticoagulant is the name for an antibody pattern that can confuse a clotting test. Like a watch that runs slow, the test tube can take longer to clot even though a person with these antibodies can have a higher risk of clots inside the body. It does not necessarily mean the person has lupus.

THE CENTRAL IDEA

In vitro: prolonged clotting.
In vivo: thrombotic risk.

DECODING THE NAME

Not necessarily lupus.
Not protective.

Lupus anticoagulant (LA) is a lab pattern caused by antibodies that can attach to proteins on fatty surfaces, such as beta-2 glycoprotein I (β₂GPI) and prothrombin. A person can have this pattern without having the disease lupus.

THE APS CONNECTION

Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is a condition in which certain lasting antibodies are linked with problems such as blood clots or particular pregnancy complications. Lab evidence can be LA or antibodies called anticardiolipin or anti-β₂GPI (types IgG or IgM). The 2006 Sydney research classification requires repeat positive tests at least 12 weeks apart, along with an appropriate clinical feature. Classification is not the same as diagnosis.

What you are looking at: The green line is the fatty surface, the pale branching shapes are antibodies, and the small labels mark the proteins they attach to. The two buttons change the story between a test tube and a body.

So what: A slow tube result and a higher risk of a clot in the body can exist together; they describe different settings.

THE DETAILS MATTER

Pre-analytics & other pitfalls

01 / Acute phase CRP may interfere with some aPTT reagents; acute illness can complicate interpretation.

02 / Factor deficiency Low factors can prolong screening tests and may correct on mixing. A mix is not definitive by itself.

03 / Sample quality Correctly fill the citrate tube to maintain the blood-to-anticoagulant ratio and prepare double-spun platelet-poor plasma per lab protocol.

Testing is best performed off interfering anticoagulation when clinically safe, or using validated DOAC removal. Never interrupt therapy solely for testing without the treating clinician's plan.
