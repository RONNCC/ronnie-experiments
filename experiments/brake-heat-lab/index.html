Lab 1 · Kinetic energy

One stop, how much energy?

Every stop turns motion into heat. Kinetic energy grows with the square of speed, so a small increase in speed costs a lot more heat. Pick a vehicle, then move the sliders. The disc shows what one stop does to a single brake.

Set the vehicle

Presets set mass, speed, disc size, and tire grip together. Sliders override any preset.

Energy scales with speed squared. From 200 to 360 km/h the energy more than triples.

Energy scales in direct proportion to mass. Loads and trailers matter.

Lab 2 · Conduction

The thermal barrier

Heat must cross a stack of layers before it reaches the brake fluid. Each layer drops the temperature by an amount set by its thickness and its thermal conductivity. Titanium conducts poorly, and that is exactly why engineers use it here. A poor conductor is a good wall.

Cross section, disc to fluid

Layer colors follow temperature. The fluid gauge compares the result with boiling lines.

Choose the piston material

Thermal conductivity in watts per metre kelvin. Lower is better here.

More flux means a hotter disc, longer stops, or a thinner barrier. Every layer drop scales with flux.

Drag the disc temperature and watch the fluid follow. The barrier drop grows as the disc warms, reaching full value near 990°C.

The lesson: With titanium the fluid sits near 167°C, safely below boiling. Swap to aluminium and the same disc pushes the fluid past 400°C. Material choice is not a detail. It is the difference between braking and not braking.

Wet versus dry: Brake fluid absorbs water over time. Wet DOT 4 fluid can boil near 155°C, far below the dry rating on the bottle. Old fluid fails earlier for this reason alone.

Lab 4 · Air density

Altitude and air

Thin air cuts both ways. Less drag means higher speeds and harder stops. Less air density also means weaker convective cooling, because the air that carries heat away is thinner. Mexico City at 2240 metres is a famous case. Denver, La Paz, and every high pass tell the same story with different numbers.

Density, cooling, and drag

Standard atmosphere model. Each curve is normalized to its sea level value.

At 2240 metres, air density is about 80 percent of sea level. Cooling falls to about 84 percent. Drag falls to 80 percent.

Synced with Lab 1. Markers show sea level, Denver near 1600 metres, Mexico City near 2240, and La Paz near 3600.

The trap: The same thin air that lets a car reach a higher speed also removes less heat at that speed. Energy in goes up. Cooling out goes down. The disc pays the difference.

Not only racing: A loaded truck crossing a high pass faces the same physics on every descent. Altitude shrinks the safety margin before the driver does anything wrong.

Lab 5 · Hydraulics

Vapor lock and the pedal

Hydraulic brakes work because liquid barely compresses. Gas compresses easily. Even a small fraction of vapor in the fluid multiplies pedal travel, because the pedal spends its motion squeezing gas instead of pushing pistons. Move the vapor slider and watch the pedal.

Pedal travel versus vapor fraction

Baseline pedal travel is 30 mm with pure liquid. Gas is about 3000 times more compressible than liquid.

With pure liquid, pedal travel is 30 mm. Add 1 percent vapor and travel grows past 900 mm. The pedal hits the floor.

One tenth of one percent of vapor already quadruples travel. Boiling does not need much gas to ruin the pedal.

Vapor lock: Once fluid boils, bubbles form in the caliper. The pedal compresses bubbles instead of moving pistons. Braking force collapses even though the pads and discs are still fine. Pumping the pedal helps only until the gas grows again.

Fade versus boil: Pad fade is gradual. Grip falls as temperature climbs, and the pedal stays firm. Boiling is sudden. The pedal goes soft or to the floor. Drivers describe the first as a long pedal and the second as no pedal at all.

Beyond racing

Why this matters everywhere

The Mexico City example is dramatic, but the same equations run on every road, rail line, and runway. Six places where brake heat decides the outcome.

Mountain grades

A loaded truck on a 6 percent grade turns gravity into heat continuously. Engine braking and retarders help, but the service brakes still soak. This is why descent signs warn about brake checks and escape ramps.

Towing and load

Extra mass raises kinetic energy in direct proportion. A car towing a trailer carries more energy into every stop, and the trailer brakes share the work only if they are set up to do so.

Track days

Repeated hard stops with short cooling gaps are the fastest way to learn about fade. Street pads fade early. Fluid condition matters as much as pad choice, because old fluid boils sooner.

City traffic

Stop and go driving rarely overheats brakes, but it wears pads and glazes surfaces. Heat cycling changes friction over months, not minutes, which is why brakes feel different in winter and summer.

Rail

Trains carry enormous kinetic energy and spread it across many discs or tread brakes. Long descents still challenge cooling, which is why dynamic braking shares the load on modern stock.

Aviation

Aircraft brakes absorb landing energy in steel discs with forced air cooling. Rejected takeoffs are the design case, because the energy is largest at the highest speed and the mass is greatest.

Heat always wins unless you give it somewhere to go.

Questions

Common questions

Why does the pedal go to the floor?

Boiling creates gas bubbles in the caliper. Gas compresses, so pedal motion squeezes bubbles instead of pushing pistons. The pedal travels far with little or no clamping force. This is vapor lock.

Why does braking get weaker before boiling?

Pad friction falls at very high temperature, a thin gas film can form between pad and disc, and hot components expand. Each effect reduces grip or changes pedal feel before the fluid ever boils.

Why titanium pistons?

Titanium conducts heat about 24 times worse than aluminium. That poor conductivity keeps heat away from the fluid. In this lab, swapping titanium for aluminium moves the fluid from safe to boiling at the same disc temperature.

Why do brake ducts matter so much?

Cooling scales with airflow. A blocked duct can cut the cooling coefficient by most of its value. The disc then soaks hotter lap after lap until the fluid follows. Lab 3 shows the divergence.

Why is altitude worse for brakes?

Thin air reduces drag, so speeds rise and stopping energy grows with the square of speed. Thin air also carries less heat away. Energy in rises while cooling out falls.

Are disc temperatures near 1000°C real?

Yes, for carbon ceramic and carbon carbon race brakes. Cast iron road discs run far cooler in normal use, typically a few hundred degrees in hard driving. The model uses the same equations for both, with different masses and materials.

What is the difference between fade and boil?

Fade is gradual grip loss from hot pads. Boil is sudden pedal loss from gas in the fluid. Fade warns you. Boil removes the brake.

Can I use this lab for my own car?

Use it to compare choices, not to certify a car. Real brake design uses measured data, fluid dynamics, and safety margins. This lab teaches the direction and size of each effect.

Plain words

Glossary

Brake fade
Gradual loss of braking grip as pads and discs get hot. The pedal stays firm, but stopping takes longer.
Vapor lock
Gas bubbles in brake fluid after boiling. The pedal compresses gas instead of pushing pistons, so braking force collapses.
Thermal barrier
The stack of pad, shim, and piston between the hot disc and the fluid. Each layer drops the temperature.
Kinetic energy
Energy of motion, one half mass times speed squared. Brakes must remove all of it as heat.
Thermal conductivity
How well a material conducts heat. Titanium is low. Aluminium is high. Low is better in a piston.
Convective cooling
Heat carried away by moving air. Faster air and denser air both help. Ducts deliver the air.
Wet boiling point
The boiling temperature of fluid that has absorbed water. Far lower than the dry rating.
Bulk modulus
Resistance to compression. Liquid is stiff. Gas is soft. Mixing them ruins pedal feel.
Heat soak
Slow temperature rise from repeated braking with too little cooling between stops.

Method and limits

How the model works

This page uses a lumped thermal model, not computational fluid dynamics. Each disc is one temperature. Cooling follows a standard forced convection scaling with air density. The barrier uses one dimensional conduction. Numbers are rounded teaching values. Treat them as a way to compare choices, not as engineering data for a specific car.
