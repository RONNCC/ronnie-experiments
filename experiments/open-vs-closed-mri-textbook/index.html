The 30 second version

Signals, not light

A strong magnet and short radio pulses make the body give off faint signals. A computer turns those signals into pictures. There are no X rays.

Two scanner shapes

Closed scanners wrap a tunnel around you and often carry stronger magnets. Open scanners leave the sides free and can feel less enclosed.

Fit decides together

The needed detail, safety screening, fit, and whether you can stay still all matter. The best scanner is the one that answers the question.

Chapter 1

How a scan makes pictures

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) makes pictures with a magnet and radio waves, not X rays. The scanner listens for tiny signals from the body and uses them to build pictures.

The short version The scanner measures signals, not light. The image is a computed map, not a photograph.

Chapter 2

Inside a closed scanner

A closed MRI is built around a round tunnel called a bore. It is open at both ends. Closed means the magnet surrounds the table on all sides.

The table slides in until the area being examined sits near the center, where the field is most even. Depending on the exam, you may enter head first or feet first, and for some exams the head stays near the opening or outside.

Many closed scanners use a superconducting magnet: a coil kept so cold that electric current flows through it without electrical resistance. This design holds a strong field, usually directed along the tunnel. Other coils send the radio pulses, listen for the response, and add the small field changes that locate the signal.

A wide bore is still a closed scanner, just with a larger opening. A shorter tunnel can also feel less enclosed.

The short version Closed describes the shape, not the strength. Wide bore and short tunnel options live inside this family.

Chapter 3

How open scanners differ

An open MRI leaves more space at the sides. A common design uses two broad plates, one above and one below the table, with the field running between them.

Some open scanners use permanent magnets, which hold a field without electric current. Others use electromagnets, including superconducting ones. The shape of the magnet and the way it makes its field are separate choices.

Some systems support the magnet on one side only. Some upright systems place magnets beside a person who sits or stands for certain exams. Not every open scanner scans upright.

Open does not promise unlimited room. The gap between the magnets and the space taken by the receiver coil still matter. Every scanner still needs a controlled field, radio pulses, a receiver, and location clues.

What the shape can offer

  • Room for staff to stand close and reach in.
  • Less enclosure around the face and body.
  • On some systems, exams while seated or standing.

What it does not promise

  • Unlimited clearance. Plates and coils still take up room.
  • The same magnet strength as a closed scanner.
  • The right protocol for every clinical question.

Chapter 4

What magnet strength buys

A tesla (T) is a unit of magnetic field strength. Closed scanners commonly use 1.5 T or 3 T. Many open scanners work below 1 T, though some open designs reach 1 T or more.

A stronger field usually makes more usable signal available. That can raise the signal to noise ratio: how clearly the wanted signal stands out from random variation. The team can spend that advantage on finer detail, faster scans, or a cleaner picture. A lower field system may repeat measurements and average them to make up for weaker signal, which takes time.

Stronger fields can also worsen some artifacts, distortions that do not represent the body, for example near metal cleared for scanning. Radio wave heating can also limit which settings are allowed.

The short version The right question is whether a scanner can produce the needed picture, not whether its number is higher.

Chapter 5

Matching the scanner to the exam

The ordering clinician starts with a question: what must the images show? The radiologist and the scanning team choose a scanner and a protocol, the set of pictures and settings needed to answer it.

Higher field closed scanners are often preferred when very fine detail or specialized techniques are needed, such as certain brain, heart, or blood vessel exams. An open scanner may suit some routine joint or spine exams when the site has the right equipment and protocol. This is not a fixed rule by body part: two exams of the same joint can ask very different questions.

Comfort is part of the match too. Movement can erase the advantage of a stronger signal, so a scan that succeeds beats a specification that looks better on paper.

Chapter 6

Comfort and positioning

Claustrophobia is fear or distress in confined spaces. Open sides or a wider, shorter tunnel can help, but a nearby upper plate or a receiver coil around the head can still feel close.

A clear explanation, a mirror where available, and easy communication with staff may also help. Useful images depend on staying still, so comfort can matter as much as equipment specifications.

For larger bodies, table weight limits and usable clearance are separate checks. The team accounts for the region being scanned, padding, and the receiver coil, not just the advertised opening.

For children, simple preparation and instructions help. A more open view does not replace deciding whether the child can stay still. Sedation, medicine that reduces alertness, needs its own safety planning and monitoring.

Only some clinical questions benefit from seated or weight bearing views, and the equipment must support the exact position and exam.

Chapter 7

What a scan feels like

Before entering the room, expect safety questions and instructions to remove items such as jewelry and electronics. A technologist positions you with cushions and places a receiver coil near the area being examined.

When contrast is part of the plan

Some exams use a contrast agent to make certain tissues or blood vessels easier to tell apart. MRI contrast is often a gadolinium based medicine injected into a vein. It changes how nearby hydrogen signals behave; it does not color the tissue. Many exams do not need it. The team weighs the benefit against risks, including past reactions and relevant kidney problems.

Chapter 8

Safety questions and myths

MRI does not use ionizing radiation, the kind of energy that can knock electrons off atoms as X rays can. It still has real risks, and safety screening matters on every scanner shape.

Myth

No X rays means no risk.

What matters instead

Strong magnets can pull certain objects toward the scanner, radio waves can heat tissue, and noise can harm hearing without protection. The main magnet stays on even between scans. Open scanners need safety screening too.

Myth

Any metal rules out MRI.

What matters instead

The team needs to know what it is and where it is. Some metals can move, heat, or distort pictures. An implant labeled MR Conditional is safe only under specified conditions, which can include field strength and settings. A lower field or open scanner is not a workaround for an unknown device. Report fragments as well as devices.

Myth

Tattoos always prevent scanning.

What matters instead

Most do not. Tattoo and permanent makeup pigments can rarely warm or irritate skin during MRI. Tell staff during screening, and speak up promptly if you feel warmth or burning during the scan.

Questions worth asking your site

  • Which scanner and protocol fit my exam, and why?
  • What should I remove or leave outside?
  • How will we talk during the scan?
  • Do I need contrast, and what does it add?
  • What happens if I feel uncomfortable or need to stop?

Before the day

  • Report every implant, device, and metal fragment.
  • Ask about options if enclosed spaces distress you.
  • Plan for the appointment to take longer than the scan itself.

Your scanning site gives the instructions that apply to you. This guide is general education, not a safety clearance.

Practice

What would change the choice?

Predict the answer, then reveal the reasoning. The point is to practice comparing requirements, not to pick a machine for a real exam.

1. Very fine detail is essential, and either design is tolerable. What is worth checking first?

Reveal the reasoning

A closed scanner is often a candidate here, but strength by itself does not establish suitability. The protocol decides.

2. Either machine can answer the question, but staying still in a tunnel is difficult. What changes?

Reveal the reasoning

Comfort can determine whether the scan succeeds. An open or wide bore option may help, and movement can erase the advantage of a stronger signal.

3. The question concerns a structure while standing. Does any open scanner work?

Reveal the reasoning

Only some systems support sitting or standing, only some questions benefit, and the equipment must support the exact position and exam.

4. An implant is labeled MR Conditional. What does the team need?

Reveal the reasoning

MR Conditional items have been shown safe only under specified conditions, which can include field strength and scan settings. The team checks the exact model, and a lower field or open scanner is not a workaround.

5. Why might a lower field system take longer to build a clean picture?

Reveal the reasoning

Averaging reduces random variation but takes time. Exam length also depends on the receiver, the settings, and whether movement forces a repeat.

Reference

Glossary

Every term in the guide, in plain words. Tap a highlighted term in a chapter to jump here.

29 terms

Pictures and signals

MRI
Magnetic resonance imaging: pictures made by measuring signals produced with magnetic fields and radio waves.
Magnetic field
The region where a magnet has an effect.
Hydrogen nucleus
The center of a hydrogen atom. MRI commonly measures signals from these nuclei in water and fat.
Radio wave
A wave that carries energy through changing electric and magnetic fields. A short burst at the right frequency tips the body's tiny magnets away from rest.
Resonance and frequency
Frequency is the rate at which a wave cycles. Resonance is the selective response to a wave at the right frequency.
Receiver coil
A wire loop or set of loops placed near the body that detects the signal.
Gradient
A controlled change in field strength across space that helps locate the signal.
Slice
A view through a thin layer of the body.
Tissue
A body material or structure, such as muscle or fat.
Signal to noise ratio
How much wanted signal there is compared with unwanted random variation.
Artifact
A feature or distortion in an image that does not faithfully represent the body.

Scanner shapes and strength

Bore
The round tunnel through a closed scanner. A wide bore is a wider tunnel, not open sides.
Closed MRI
A scanner whose magnet surrounds the table on all sides, usually as a tunnel.
Open MRI
A scanner that leaves more space at the sides, often with plates above and below the table.
Upright MRI
A system that supports some scans while a person sits or stands.
Field strength and tesla (T)
How strong the magnetic field is, measured in units called tesla.
Electromagnet
A magnet made by running electric current through coils.
Permanent magnet
A magnet that holds its field without electric current.
Superconducting magnet
An electromagnet whose very cold coil carries current without electrical resistance.

People and planning

Protocol
The set of pictures and scanner settings planned for a particular exam.
Radiologist
A doctor trained to interpret medical images.
Technologist
The professional who operates the scanner and helps position the person.

Comfort and safety

Claustrophobia
Fear or distress in confined spaces.
Sedation
Medicine used to reduce alertness or help someone stay still, under appropriate supervision.
Contrast agent
A substance used to change how structures appear in the image. Many MRI exams do not need it.
Gadolinium
A metallic element used in bound form in many MRI contrast medicines.
Ionizing radiation
Energy that can remove electrons from atoms. MRI does not use this kind of radiation.
Implant and MR Conditional
An implant is a device or material placed inside the body. MR Conditional means an item has been shown safe only under specified MRI conditions.
Weight bearing
Supporting body weight, as when standing, while the structure being examined is imaged.
