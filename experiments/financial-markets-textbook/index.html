CHAPTER 01 · FOUNDATION

The financial market foundation

A financial market is a way to exchange claims: who can use resources now, who must repay later, and what those promises are worth today.

Eight ideas that make the rest legible

  • Asset: something valuable that an entity owns or a claim it can enforce, such as cash, a bond, a loan receivable or a share.
  • Debt: a promise to repay principal and usually interest. The lender holds an asset; the borrower records a matching liability.
  • Equity: an ownership claim after the entity's debts. A share's return is not fixed and may rise or fall with the business and market.
  • Money: a widely accepted means of payment and unit for quoting value. A bank deposit is a bank's promise to pay; cash and central bank reserves are different liabilities.
  • Market price: a price at which a particular quantity could trade under particular conditions. The latest price may not be available for a large order.
  • Balance sheet: a snapshot of assets, liabilities and equity. Assets equal liabilities plus equity as an accounting identity.
  • Liquidity: the ability to pay when due or trade an asset quickly without a large price concession. Funding liquidity and market liquidity are related but not identical.
  • Risk: the possibility that an outcome differs from expectation, including loss, delayed payment, failed operations or a loss of access to funding.

WORKED EXAMPLE · A SIMPLE BOND

A company borrows $1,000 for one year and promises $50 interest. The investor's asset is the company's debt. The company's liability is $1,000 plus the agreed interest due. If the company cannot pay, the promise still exists, but its market value may fall.

Borrower

Bank

Why price can change: expected repayment, risk, interest rates, time, supply and demand, and resaleability all matter, and in real markets they move together rather than one at a time.

Check your understanding: Is a bank deposit the same thing as cash?

No. A deposit is a commercial bank liability. Cash is a central bank liability. They are designed to be exchangeable at par in normal circumstances, but they are not the same legal claim. See Chapter 7.

  • A financial instrument is a claim, not just a label on a screen.
  • Every asset is someone else's liability or ownership claim.
  • Liquidity describes how easily an obligation can be met or an asset traded.

CHAPTER 02 · FIRST DEEP UNIT

FX: a price between two monies

Foreign exchange, or FX, is the market for exchanging one currency claim for another. A quote has a direction, a unit, a price and two payment legs.

Read quote direction before calculating

In USD/KRW, the first currency is the base currency and the second is the quote currency. The number says how many units of the second currency buy one unit of the first. Thus USD/KRW 1,350 means 1 USD = 1,350 KRW. A rise to 1,400 means each dollar costs more won: the dollar strengthened against the won under this quote convention.

TRY IT · DIRECTION AND UNITS

Buy USD 100,000; pay KRW 135,000,000. Quote unit: KRW per USD.

Arithmetic only. This ignores bid/ask spread, fees, credit, taxes, settlement arrangements and real price movement. No market data is used.

Spot, forward and FX swap

  • Spot: agree a currency exchange now for settlement on the pair's spot value date. That date is often two business days later, but conventions vary by currency pair, holidays and product.
  • Forward: agree now to exchange currencies on a later date at a rate set now. The rate reflects interest-rate differentials and market pricing, not a simple guess about the future spot rate.
  • FX swap: exchange two currencies on one date and reverse the exchange on a different date, with both rates agreed at the start. It is a common way to borrow one currency against another for a period.

A commercial FX swap is a market contract between counterparties. A central bank currency swap line is a standing or temporary agreement between central banks that may let one central bank provide another with foreign-currency liquidity. The line itself is not the same thing as a firm borrowing dollars from a dealer. See Chapter 3.

WORKED TWO LEG EXAMPLE · ILLUSTRATIVE

A Korean importer owes a US supplier $100,000. At the assumed rate of KRW 1,350 per USD, the importer needs KRW 135,000,000 before fees. It buys dollars from its bank. At settlement, the buyer's bank must deliver the won leg and receive the dollar leg. The exact accounts, intermediaries and settlement method depend on the transaction.

Who owes what, and when?

At execution, each party has a contractual obligation. Before settlement, one side is exposed if it pays its currency and never receives the other. On the value date, payment systems or correspondent accounts move the two claims.

Check your understanding: USD/KRW moves from 1,350 to 1,400. What happened?

One dollar now buys more won. The dollar strengthened against the won, or the won weakened against the dollar, measured in KRW per USD. The opposite direction would be described differently under the reciprocal quote.

  • USD/KRW names won per dollar, not dollars per won.
  • Spot, forward and FX swap differ by timing and leg structure.
  • Every deliverable FX trade creates two currency obligations.

CHAPTER 03 · POLICY AND BALANCE SHEETS

Central bank intervention

When a central bank intervenes, it trades claims with counterparties. It does not send a bag of digital tokens into a market or conjure foreign currency at will.

Two routes to dollar liquidity

Sell an existing reserve asset. A central bank or government sells dollars or a dollar asset to a dealer or other counterparty and receives local currency. This uses an asset it already holds. A sale of a US Treasury security may first require selling or pledging that security, then moving dollars through a bank and payment system.

Borrow dollars through a swap arrangement. In a central bank liquidity swap, two central banks exchange currencies at the prevailing rate and agree to reverse the exchange later, often at the same exchange rate plus an interest payment. The receiving central bank can then lend the dollars to banks in its jurisdiction, often through an auction. Its bank loan is a separate transaction, and the central bank remains liable to return dollars to the other central bank. The Fed's description of the arrangement makes these separate transactions explicit [S4].

The Bank of Korea has published examples of competitive dollar loan facility auctions funded with dollar proceeds of swap transactions with the Federal Reserve [S5]. An auction allocates offered funding under stated terms. It is not a market price guarantee.

Sterilized versus unsterilized

The second table above shows a sterilized sale: the central bank offsets the drained reserve balances with a domestic bond purchase, so its foreign asset becomes a domestic bond asset and bank reserves end unchanged. Without that offsetting operation the sale is unsterilized and bank reserves fall. The exact accounting and monetary effect depend on the institutional setup, transaction and operating framework.

Central bank intervention can affect expectations, liquidity and portfolio choices, but it cannot dictate the exchange rate by itself. Its impact depends on scale, market conditions, credibility, policy consistency and the willingness of market participants to trade.

Check your understanding: Can a central bank create unlimited dollars to defend its currency?

No. A central bank can issue its own currency under its legal and policy framework. It cannot create another country's currency at will. It needs foreign assets, borrowing, swap access or future foreign currency receipts to make foreign currency payments.

  • Intervention is an exchange of claims with counterparties.
  • FX reserves are not bank reserve balances at the central bank.
  • A swap line, a central bank auction and a dealer trade are distinct transactions.
  • Intervention's price effect is possible, not guaranteed.

CHAPTER 04 · HOW A PRICE FORMS

Execution and price discovery

A quote is a willingness to trade on stated terms. An execution occurs when the parties agree to the trade. Neither event proves that payment has settled.

Three common ways a trade gets done

  • Bilateral OTC or RFQ: OTC means over the counter; RFQ means request for quote. A customer requests a quote from one or more dealers, then agrees terms with one. The deal is private and depends on the parties' credit and relationship.
  • Dealer internalization: a dealer matches customer interest against its own inventory or other customer flow instead of immediately hedging every trade externally.
  • Electronic matching: orders or quotes interact through an electronic system. That may be a single dealer platform, a multi dealer platform, a broker's system or an exchange. The rules and participants differ.

Spot and many FX derivatives trade OTC rather than on one central exchange. The BIS describes FX as decentralized and fragmented, with dealers, multiple execution methods and a broad set of electronic venues [S1]. Exchange traded contracts are another route, not the universal path for institutional trades.

Bid, ask, spread and depth

The bid is the price at which a dealer is willing to buy; the ask is the price at which it is willing to sell. The spread is ask minus bid, in the quote's units. If a dealer quotes USD/KRW 1,348.8 bid and 1,350.2 ask, the dealer buys dollars at 1,348.8 won and sells dollars at 1,350.2 won. A customer buying USD generally pays the ask.

An order book lists resting buy and sell orders in a matching venue. Market depth describes how much quantity is available near the current price. A quoted top price might only be available for a small size. A large order can consume several price levels.

Slippage is the difference between an expected execution price and the actual average execution price. It can come from price movement, spread, market impact, routing choices, latency or thin depth. Splitting an order may lower market impact, but can increase time exposed to price changes and handling costs.

ILLUSTRATIVE ORDER EXAMPLE

A buy order for 10 million USD encounters only 4 million available at KRW 1,350.0, then 6 million at KRW 1,351.0. Its weighted average is (4 × 1,350 + 6 × 1,351) ÷ 10 = KRW 1,350.6 per USD. The displayed best ask was not the price for the whole amount.

Execution may happen in milliseconds. A trade may still wait hours or days for its value date, then need operational steps before payment finality. Fast agreement is not fast settlement.

Check your understanding: Does a dealer quote promise unlimited quantity at that price?

No. Quotes may be firm or indicative and can carry size, duration, credit, platform and market conditions. Always ask what amount the price covers and whether it is executable.

  • A price can be formed bilaterally or through varied electronic mechanisms.
  • Bid and ask are from the quoting dealer's point of view.
  • Large orders may face spread, market impact and slippage.
  • Execution is agreement; settlement is a later process.

CHAPTER 05 · OPERATIONS

From trade to settlement

The lifecycle turns an agreed deal into matched instructions, funded payments and discharged obligations. Each stage has its own checks and failure modes.

  1. Pre trade checks. Verify trading authority, counterparty, credit limits, product eligibility, currency, amount, tenor and available funding. Policies may block or resize an order.
  2. Execution. Record what the parties agreed: instrument, direction, price, notional, value date, counterparty and any special terms.
  3. Capture. Enter or electronically receive the trade in internal systems. A correct conversation can still be captured incorrectly.
  4. Confirmation and matching. Both parties compare material terms. Differences such as amount, date, price, account or settlement instructions require repair.
  5. Netting and funding. If a valid netting arrangement applies, many obligations may be reduced to a net amount. Treasury arranges currency liquidity. Netting is bounded by the agreement, product, entities, jurisdiction and system.
  6. Value date and settlement. Parties instruct payments or securities movements. Settlement finality is the point at which the transfer is legally and operationally final under the applicable system rules.

Value date is not the processing queue

A trade's value date is the date on which its payment obligations are due. For FX spot, that is often T+2, where T is trade date, but conventions depend on the currency pair, holidays and product. Some pairs use T+1 or same day; a holiday in either currency center may shift the date. BIS survey definitions include spot transactions settling within two business days and identify exceptions and pair calendars [S2].

The period between trade and value date is not the time a payment sits in a slow wire queue. On value date, payments can settle quickly once sent and accepted by the chosen system, subject to operating hours, liquidity and controls. A next day payment and an instantaneous payment are not the same thing as a next day trade.

Check your understanding: If a trade is confirmed, has it settled?

No. Confirmation means the parties agree on the trade details. Settlement requires payment or delivery under the relevant system or account arrangement.

  • Execution, capture, confirmation, funding and settlement are distinct.
  • Value date tells you when obligations are due, not how long a payment system takes.
  • Pair calendars and contracts determine the actual FX value date.
  • There is no single universal sequence of payment systems.

CHAPTER 06 · PAYMENT SYSTEMS

The settlement plumbing

Messages, correspondent accounts and settlement systems do different jobs. The route depends on currency, banks, membership, liquidity and operating hours.

Messaging is not settlement

SWIFT is a secure financial messaging network and standards community. It does not hold funds or run accounts for customers; its messages can facilitate payments but do not themselves move the account balances [S3]. A message can be sent, delayed, rejected, repaired or acted upon depending on the institutions and rules.

Three USD settlement paths

  • Fedwire Funds: a Federal Reserve real time gross settlement service. Eligible participants instruct transfers between accounts at Reserve Banks. Transfers are immediate, final and irrevocable once processed [S6].
  • CHIPS: a privately operated high value USD clearing and settlement system. It combines continuous netting and liquidity saving mechanisms with final settlement through the operating day; it is not simply a slow wire. See its public disclosure and system description [S7].
  • Correspondent banking: one bank holds an account with another, often in a foreign currency. From the account holder's view it may be called a nostro account, “our account with you”; from the servicing bank's view it may be called a vostro, “your account with us”. Payment is a debit and credit of bank claims, not a physical cross border transfer of cash.

CLS and payment versus payment

Payment versus payment, or PvP, makes the final transfer of one currency conditional on the final transfer of the other. CLSSettlement provides PvP settlement for eligible FX trades. The June 2026 BIS review describes CLS as settling 18 currencies, including KRW as of its currency list [S8]. Currency eligibility does not mean every trade, participant, product, date or payment can use CLS.

CLS is not a central counterparty that replaces the original FX counterparties. PvP reduces principal or Herstatt risk: the risk of paying away one currency without receiving the other. It does not eliminate market risk, liquidity risk, replacement cost if a trade is replaced, operational risk or every counterparty exposure.

There is no universal “SWIFT → Fedwire → CHIPS → CLS” pipeline. A particular transaction may use messages plus a bank's correspondent account; another may settle its USD leg through Fedwire or CHIPS; an eligible FX transaction may use CLS PvP; banks can also settle among their own accounts. Routes depend on memberships, correspondents, funding, timing and rules.

Check your understanding: Does SWIFT settle the dollar leg?

No. SWIFT transmits standardized financial messages. The dollar ledger transfer or settlement may occur through a bank's accounts, Fedwire, CHIPS or another relevant arrangement.

  • Messages communicate instructions; settlement systems transfer ledger claims.
  • Fedwire and CHIPS have distinct models, not a fast and slow version of one wire.
  • CLS PvP protects the exchange of eligible currency legs, but is not a CCP or a cure for all risk.

CHAPTER 07 · MONEY AND CREDIT

Fiat money and its backing

Most modern money is a promise recorded on a balance sheet. Different issuers create different claims, and institutions make those claims usable together.

Three different liabilities

  • Banknotes: central bank liabilities that the public can hold.
  • Central bank reserves: electronic balances held by eligible institutions at the central bank. They are commonly used to settle payments between banks.
  • Commercial bank deposits: liabilities of commercial banks to households and firms. They are not central bank money just because a phone app shows a digital number.

The Bank of England explains that commercial bank lending typically creates a matching bank deposit, while banks face constraints such as capital, funding, liquidity, risk management, regulation and the price of credit [S9]. A bank does not simply lend out a fixed pile of deposits, nor can it lend without limit. When a customer pays someone at another bank, the banks ultimately settle between themselves, often using central bank reserves or other established arrangements.

Bank makes a $100 loan

Asset: +$100 loan claim

Liability: +$100 customer deposit

Borrower receives the loan

Asset: +$100 deposit

Liability: +$100 loan owed

What gives fiat money value?

Fiat money is money not redeemable at a fixed rate for a commodity such as gold. Its acceptance rests on a framework: the state's legal and tax system, monetary and fiscal institutions, productive capacity, stable payments and public confidence. Central bank assets support policy credibility, but they are not a fixed gold-redemption promise for each unit of currency.

Inflation reduces the amount of goods and services a unit buys over time. If confidence in purchasing power weakens, households and firms may alter prices, wages, saving, borrowing and currency choices. Monetary policy can influence conditions but cannot create productive resources by accounting entry alone. Fiscal institutions, law, political legitimacy and the real economy matter too.

A central bank can create its own domestic currency liabilities within its mandate and operating framework. It cannot produce foreign currency at will. Its ability to meet external obligations depends on foreign reserves, market access, agreements and future foreign currency cash flows.

Check your understanding: Are a household deposit and a bank's reserve balance both money?

They are both monetary claims, but not the same kind. A household holds a claim on its commercial bank. The commercial bank holds a claim on the central bank. Households generally cannot hold commercial bank reserve accounts directly.

  • Money is a set of issuer liabilities, not one undifferentiated digital balance.
  • Commercial bank lending creates deposits and a matching loan claim.
  • Fiat value depends on institutions, productive economy and confidence, not fixed gold convertibility.
  • Domestic money creation is not foreign currency creation.

CHAPTER 08 · THE FIRST DEEP UNIT'S CREDIT ENGINE

Prime brokerage and leverage

A prime broker is a bank that provides services and credit to active investment firms: custody, financing, securities lending, execution and risk controls. Leverage means using borrowed capital to support a larger position.

Services and legal relationship

Prime brokerage can include custody, financing long positions, securities lending for shorts, centralized reporting, execution access, trade give ups, portfolio margin and FX prime brokerage. A give up means a trade is executed with one counterparty or venue and then booked to another institution, subject to agreed procedures and credit acceptance. Exact services vary by agreement and jurisdiction.

Before extending credit, a bank reviews the client's financial condition, strategy, legal entities, collateral, liquidity and potential losses. Master agreements and collateral documents specify what may be netted, what collateral qualifies, how disputes are handled and what happens after default. A trading screen is not a promise of unlimited credit.

Cash loan or synthetic exposure?

With a cash margin loan, the client buys an asset partly with borrowed money, and the prime broker may hold the asset as collateral. In a total return swap (TRS), the client receives the economic return on a reference asset from a dealer and pays financing and other agreed amounts. The dealer may hedge by holding or shorting the reference shares. The client can get market exposure without owning the shares directly, but still faces margin, contract and counterparty obligations.

ILLUSTRATIVE LEVERAGE EXAMPLE

A fund has $20m of equity and borrows $80m to buy $100m of securities. Assets are $100m, debt is $80m and equity is $20m. At entry, gross exposure is $100m, net exposure is also $100m if there are no shorts, and leverage measured as gross assets divided by equity is 5×. A 10% price fall removes $10m, half the fund's original equity, before costs, hedges or margin changes.

Collateral is not a magic shield

  • Collateral: assets pledged to support an obligation.
  • Haircut: a discount applied to collateral value so a lender does not count every dollar at face value. A $100 asset with a 10% haircut may count as $90 of collateral value.
  • Initial margin: collateral required when establishing a position, intended to cover potential future exposure over a closeout period.
  • Variation margin: collateral transferred as the mark to market changes, commonly to cover current exposure.
  • Notional: reference amount used to calculate contractual payments. Notional is not automatically the amount at risk or the amount a firm borrowed.

Potential future exposure estimates how much a counterparty's exposure might grow before a position can be closed. Concentration risk arises when positions depend heavily on the same issuer, sector, factor or exit route. Wrong way risk occurs when the value of collateral or the counterparty's ability to pay worsens at the same time the exposure grows. Gross exposure counts positions before offsets; net exposure accounts for permitted offsets and hedges under a stated method.

Check your understanding: A TRS has $500m notional. Is the client automatically $500m in credit debt?

No. Notional scales the contract's return calculation. The current replacement value, potential future exposure, margin, enforceable netting and stress losses are needed to assess credit exposure. Do not confuse reference notional with loan balance or possible loss.

  • Prime brokerage bundles services with a conditional credit relationship.
  • Cash financing and synthetic swaps create different legal claims.
  • Collateral has haircuts, liquidity and concentration limits.
  • Notional, exposure and loss are different quantities.

CHAPTER 09 · WHAT CAN GO WRONG

Risk controls and liquidation

Risk controls make credit exposure visible and actionable. They reduce the chance and cost of failure; they cannot promise that losses stop at the modelled limit.

Limits and monitoring are a chain

  • Pre trade limits may cap counterparty exposure, notional, concentration, product, tenor or loss under stress.
  • Marks and intraday calls compare current value and collateral under an agreement. A margin call must be communicated, disputed or agreed, funded and transferred.
  • Stress tests ask how a portfolio behaves under severe but plausible price moves, liquidity shocks, volatility changes and correlated losses.
  • Liquidity and concentration limits consider whether positions can actually be unwound and whether collateral remains saleable under stress.
  • Escalation and governance assign people authority to challenge, reduce or exit exposure. A risk alert without a decision and follow through is not a control.

Real time monitoring does not imply universal instant remargining or guaranteed automatic protection. Contracts specify call times, thresholds, dispute processes, eligible collateral and remedies. Netting only applies within enforceable agreements and legally recognized sets; it does not magically offset every exposure across every bank.

Losses have different meanings

A client may lose money on a security or swap. The dealer may separately have a hedge that gains or loses value. A counterparty credit loss is the unpaid amount after considering collateral, enforceable netting and recoveries. A market loss on the hedge is a separate P&L item. Counting the whole notional as both counterparty loss and hedge loss would double count.

Suppose a dealer's exposure to a client after netting is $12m, it holds $8m of collateral after haircuts, and it later recovers $1m. A simple credit loss is $3m ($12m − $8m − $1m), before additional legal and closeout effects. This invented example is not the same as a $12m market loss or $12m notional.

Check your understanding: Does a margin call guarantee there can be no loss?

No. The client may not pay in time, the market can gap, the collateral may be hard to sell or worth less than expected, and closeout can take time. Margin reduces but does not remove exposure.

  • Controls require data, authority, escalation and action.
  • Calls and liquidations are contractual processes, not universal instant switches.
  • Credit exposure is not notional; hedge P&L is not the same as client default loss.
  • A gap can cross collateral even when monitoring is active.

CHAPTER 10 · DOCUMENTED CASE

Archegos, March 2021

Archegos was an equity and total return swap prime brokerage failure, not an FX crash. Its collapse shows how leverage, concentration, fragmented information and weak governance can combine.

Documented sequence

Before March 2021

Archegos accumulated concentrated, leveraged positions, including through equity swaps. Credit Suisse's Special Committee report describes repeated risk limit breaches, under margining and known warning signs.

March 22–24

Falling prices, including a 6.7% drop in ViacomCBS on March 22, cut the portfolio value. The Credit Suisse report says more than $600m of excess margin at CS was wiped out by March 24; Archegos paid a $175m call that day, while CS prepared a much larger call for the next day.

March 25–26

CS issued two calls totaling more than $2.8bn on March 25. Archegos told its brokers it lacked liquidity to meet the calls. On March 26, CS issued an Event of Default notice and began unwinding the positions. Other brokers also moved to reduce exposures.

After default

Losses differed by broker based on its exposure, collateral, timing and execution. Credit Suisse reported about $5.5 billion of losses from the default and unwind, according to its published independent investigation [S10]. This is Credit Suisse's loss, not Archegos's total market notional.

Why the same trade looked different to each bank

Each prime broker had its own contract, collateral, position and limits. Positions were booked through swaps that could create economic exposure without the fund appearing as the direct owner of every share. A bank could understand its own exposure but not necessarily the combined positions across all other prime brokers.

The Credit Suisse investigation describes concentrated and volatile positions, persistent limit breaches, insufficient margin, failures to act on information, management and risk escalation problems, and underinvestment in risk management. The report's findings are about Credit Suisse's relationship and conduct, not a complete audit of every firm's decisions [S10].

A simplified loss waterfall

1Client cannot payMissed variation margin or closeout amount
2Use collateralValue can be reduced by haircuts, gaps and costs

Archegos was not simply a computer that reacted too slowly. The report points to known warning signals, repeated limit breaches, insufficient response and governance weaknesses. Faster data does not help when the people with authority do not act on it.

DOCUMENTED FACT VS TEACHING EXAMPLE

Documented: Credit Suisse's independent board committee report, dated July 29, 2021, states approximately $5.5 billion in losses from Archegos's default and unwind. Not asserted here: a total Archegos notional, exact cross-broker exposure, or one universal loss number for the whole street. The schematic only explains mechanisms.

Check your understanding: Why could several prime brokers lose money on the same fund?

Each broker had a separate exposure and its own collateral and liquidation. The fund's positions were concentrated in shared securities. When clients failed to meet calls, multiple banks tried to reduce exposure, potentially selling similar shares into a falling market. Recoveries varied with each broker's exposure and actions.

  • Archegos was an equity TRS and prime brokerage case.
  • Several brokers could each have exposure to the same concentrated risk.
  • Credit Suisse's approximately $5.5bn was its loss, not the fund's total notional.
  • Governance and ignored signals mattered alongside market moves and technology.

CHAPTER 11 · INTEGRATION

The full trade, end to end

Unusual but valid teaching assumption: a US importer already holds KRW 13.5 billion from earlier receipts and owes a Korean supplier USD 10 million. It buys USD 10 million from its bank for KRW 13.5 billion at an illustrative KRW 1,350 per USD, receives the dollars, and then instructs its bank to pay the supplier in USD. This isolates quote direction and settlement; it is not meant to imply that Korean suppliers usually invoice in dollars. Every number is illustrative.

Worked institutional USD/KRW purchase

  1. Business need: the importer holds KRW 13,500,000,000 and owes the supplier USD 10,000,000 in five business days.
  2. Pre trade: the importer and its bank confirm authority, credit, the payment's currency, amount and date. The bank checks its own limits and funding.
  3. Execution: the importer requests a quote. Assume the bank offers KRW 1,350 per USD for the requested amount. They agree. The quote is illustrative, not a market price claim.
  4. Obligation: under this exchange, the importer delivers KRW 13,500,000,000 and receives USD 10,000,000. The selling bank has the opposite contractual legs. Other dealers or internal positions may change which entity finally holds the risk.
  5. Capture and confirm: each side records the trade and checks amount, direction, price, counterparty and date. Mismatched instructions are repaired before settlement.
  6. Fund and settle the FX trade: on the agreed value date, the importer provides the won through its bank and receives the dollars from the selling bank. The two currency legs could use eligible PvP or other bank and payment-system routes. Then pay the invoice: the importer instructs its bank to send USD 10,000,000 to the supplier; correspondent and payment-system arrangements deliver and credit the payment.
  7. Close: once the contractual payments are final, the FX obligations are discharged. A payment message alone would not prove that all balances have settled.

ARITHMETIC CHECK

$10,000,000 × 1,350 KRW/USD = KRW 13,500,000,000. Units cancel to KRW. If the quote instead meant USD per KRW, the calculation would be different. The quote convention is part of the economics.

Separate scenario · a leveraged firm fails

This failure example is deliberately separate from the FX invoice. A fund has $20m of equity, borrows $80m and owns $100m of concentrated shares, so initial equity is 20% of position value. A 15% fall reduces the shares to $85m while the debt is still $80m. Equity falls to $5m before costs. If the broker requires more collateral, the fund may not have it. A further 8% gap on the $85m position costs $6.8m, leaving negative equity after the gap, before liquidation costs. The broker can lose money if collateral and recoveries do not cover its contractual exposure.

The fund does not owe the whole notional as a loss: market moves erode equity, while margin terms, loan balances, hedge values, collateral and closeout determine the actual counterparty credit loss. This is the interaction Archegos illustrates, though these figures are invented, not Archegos facts.

Concept map: five risks that interact

Market riskprices move
↔
Liquidity riskcash or exit is scarce
↔
Credit riskan obligation is unpaid
↔

↔
Systemic riskstress spreads between firms

Prices can fall and trigger margin calls. Funding can disappear while the client tries to meet them. A payment can fail while one side has already delivered. Liquidations can push prices lower and impair other counterparties. The risks are distinct, but a shock can pass through them in sequence.

Final check: Why is the first map about claims rather than a pipe of money?

Because most finance is about legal and accounting claims between institutions. Market participants agree obligations; banks record deposits and loans; settlement systems transfer balances between ledgers. The exact route varies, and no single pipe describes every trade.

  • Read the quote, state the unit, then calculate both currency legs.
  • Follow an obligation from agreement to final payment.
  • Market loss, counterparty credit loss and notional are distinct.
  • Liquidity, credit, settlement, market and systemic risk can reinforce one another.

Glossary

Short, plain language definitions. A precise legal meaning can vary by contract, market rules and jurisdiction.

Asset
A resource or enforceable claim that has value to its holder. See Chapter 1.
Debt
A contractual promise to repay borrowed money, often with interest.
Money
A widely accepted means of payment and a unit for quoting value. Money comes in claims issued by different institutions.
Market price
The value at which a particular quantity may trade under current market conditions; not a promise about future value.
Risk
The possibility that outcomes differ from expectations, including market loss, missed payment, illiquidity or operational failure.
Balance sheet
A snapshot of assets, liabilities and equity; assets equal liabilities plus equity.
FX
Foreign exchange: trading one currency for another.
Forward
An agreement made now to exchange currencies at a later date at a rate set now.
Bid and ask
The price at which a dealer is willing to buy and the price at which it is willing to sell. The spread is the difference.
Ask
The price at which a dealer is willing to sell. A customer buying from the dealer generally pays the ask.
Spread
The difference between the bid and ask prices.
Collateral
Cash or assets pledged to support an obligation.
Correspondent bank
A bank that provides account or payment services to another bank, often in a currency the second bank does not issue.
Counterparty credit risk
The risk that the other party cannot meet its contractual obligation, leaving an exposure after netting, collateral and recovery.
Equity
An ownership claim after an entity's liabilities. Also, in a financed position, assets less liabilities.
Fiat money
Currency not redeemable at a fixed commodity amount; acceptance depends on institutions and confidence.
SWIFT
A secure financial messaging network and standards provider. It does not itself hold or settle customer funds.
Fedwire
The Federal Reserve Banks’ real time gross settlement service for eligible high value dollar transfers.
CHIPS
A privately operated high value USD clearing and settlement system with continuous netting and liquidity saving features.
CLS
CLSSettlement is a payment versus payment service for eligible FX transactions.
Value date
The agreed date on which payment or delivery obligations are due.
FX swap
A pair of currency exchanges for different value dates, agreed together at the start.
Haircut
A percentage discount applied to collateral value when calculating how much credit it supports.
Herstatt risk
FX settlement principal risk: one party pays the currency it sold but fails to receive the currency it bought.
Potential future exposure
An estimate of how much exposure to a counterparty may grow before a position can be closed.
Concentration risk
Risk from relying heavily on a small number of issuers, factors, counterparties or exit routes.
Initial margin
Collateral collected when opening a position to cover potential future exposure over a closeout period.
Liquidity
The ability to meet payments when due, or trade an asset quickly without a large price concession.
Market depth
How much quantity is available to trade at or near current prices.
Market risk
The risk that market prices or rates move against a position.
Netting
Combining obligations so only a net amount is due, where the contract, entities and law permit.
Leverage
The relationship between a position's size and the capital supporting it; one common measure is gross assets divided by equity.
Margin call
A request under an agreement to post more collateral or reduce exposure after a threshold or valuation changes.
Gross exposure
The size of positions before subtracting permitted offsets or hedges under a stated method.
Net exposure
Exposure after defined offsets or hedges are applied; the result depends on the measurement and legal netting set.
Notional
A reference amount used to calculate a contract's payments. It is not automatically the debt, exposure or potential loss.
Nostro / vostro
Related labels for a bank account relationship: “our account with you” from one side and “your account with us” from the other.
RFQ
Request for quote: a customer asks one or more dealers for an executable price on stated terms.
Give up
A trade executed with one party or venue and, under an agreed process, booked to another institution that accepts it.
OTC
Over the counter: a trade agreed directly between parties or through a dealer or platform, rather than necessarily on an exchange.
Prime broker
A bank that provides financing, custody, securities lending, execution access and related services to investment firms.
Payment versus payment (PvP)
A settlement method where a payment in one currency occurs if and only if payment in the other currency occurs.
Reserves
Balances commercial banks hold at the central bank. Different from foreign exchange reserves held by a central bank or government.
Settlement finality
The point at which a transfer is final and irrevocable under the relevant system's rules and law.
Slippage
The gap between an expected execution price and the actual average execution price.
Spot
An FX transaction agreed now for settlement on the currency pair's spot value date, often but not always T+2.
Sterilization
A follow up monetary operation designed to offset the domestic reserve balance effect of an FX operation.
Total return swap (TRS)
A contract that exchanges the economic return on a reference asset for financing and other agreed payments.
Variation margin
Collateral transferred as the market value of an existing position changes.
Wrong way risk
When a counterparty's capacity to pay or collateral value deteriorates at the same time exposure to that counterparty rises.
