Start here / The essentials

01

The company, in plain English.

Coretsu sits at the intersection of AI, public information, and the federal government. Its public website is unusually brief, so the most useful preparation is to distinguish what is known from what is still a question.

Company statement

What it says it does

Coretsu describes itself as “applied technology for the U.S. Government,” deployed at the Pentagon, Congress, and the Office of Management and Budget (OMB). The site does not specify the scope, users, or contract arrangements of those deployments.

Open questions

What we cannot establish

Public sources reviewed do not establish employee count, revenue, product adoption, underlying model providers, a complete customer list, or the interview process. Don't assume a particular role or a particular technology stack.

A credible answer to “What do you know about us?”

“My understanding is that Coretsu builds applied technology for government. Your site says it is deployed at the Pentagon, Congress, and OMB; the FedRAMP listing describes a government-information platform that uses AI and public databases. I'm interested in how you turn that information into something genuinely useful and trustworthy in a high-stakes workflow—and I'd love to understand which problems this team owns.”

From a question to an answer.

The FedRAMP listing offers a rare glimpse of the product's intended flow. This is a simplified rendering of its vendor-supplied description—not a claim about Coretsu's internal architecture.

Its listed product website currently presents a “Sherpa” sign-in with CAC/PIV and “Authorized access only.” The public sign-in alone does not establish Sherpa's features, its relationship to the suite, or who uses it. Do not attempt unauthorized access.

Why this could matter

Hypothesis: a policy or agency professional often needs to find, compare, and explain scattered information quickly. A useful assistant would save research time while preserving provenance, permissions, and the user's ability to verify a consequential answer. Ask Coretsu which users and workflows matter most.

Know the authorization distinction

The FedRAMP listing shows Initial Implementation and Agency Auth In Process as of June 8, 2026; it displays zero authorizations, Rev5 / Agency / Class D (High), and no certified-since date. That is not the same as a completed authorization or FedRAMP certification. Status may change; check the live record before the interview. An agency authority to operate (ATO) concerns an agency's risk decision; it is not a guarantee that a product is risk-free.

The responsible reading

Disclosures and lobbying are not evidence of wrongdoing; neither are they proof of product adoption. Likewise, a product listing, an agency deployment, and a contract award are different things. Keep the entity, date, and source scope attached to each claim.

Due diligence before an offer

  • Product reality: Which workflows are live, who uses them, and how do pilots convert to sustained use?
  • Security: What data is processed, where is it hosted, who can access it, and what is the current authorization scope?
  • Commercial health: What are the revenue mix, runway, customer concentration, and funding situation? What can be shared under appropriate confidentiality?
  • Operating model: How much is software product versus bespoke delivery? How are roadmap conflicts resolved?
  • Role: Which legal entity employs me? Where is the team located? Are citizenship, clearance or on-site requirements applicable?
  • Responsible use: What uses are out of bounds? How are accuracy, civil liberties, privacy, and incident escalation governed?
  • Offer: Ask for equity type, fully diluted denominator, vesting, exercise window, salary, benefits and any restrictions in writing.
  • Governance: Who are the leaders and board, what changed recently, and how are conflicts of interest managed?

Context / The public-sector market

04

Why selling to government is different.

Market context

The user isn't always the buyer

End users, mission owners, security teams, contracting officers, and budget holders may each evaluate a different part of the decision. A good product must serve the actual workflow and survive an institutional buying process.

Market context

Trust is part of the product

Identity, access control, auditability, data handling, accessibility, and security review are not just paperwork. They shape what can be deployed and whether a user will trust an answer.

Market context

Alternatives are varied

Government buyers can compare general-purpose productivity assistants, internal tools, search and data vendors, integrators, and doing the work manually. Don't assume any specific competitor is in a particular procurement.

Market context

Measure mission outcomes

Time saved is useful but incomplete. Also consider citation accuracy, task completion, user confidence, security incidents, accessibility, and whether errors change consequential decisions.

Practice out loud / Answer frameworks

06

Make the interview count.

Use these prompts as rehearsal, not a script. The answers are examples; replace them with your own experience and never claim work you haven't done.

CompanyWhat does Coretsu do?

“The company describes applied technology for the U.S. government, including deployments at the Pentagon, Congress and OMB. The marketplace describes an AI-enhanced public-information platform. I'm curious about the team's specific users, workflows, and outcomes.”

Why it works: it is specific without inventing product details.

MotivationWhy Coretsu?

“I'm drawn to the challenge of making complex information useful in important decisions. The opportunity to combine practical delivery, user trust, and a difficult operating environment is compelling. In [your real example], I [your action] and learned [your lesson]. I'd like to bring that approach here.”

ProductHow would you find the right first use case?

Interview users; map a repeatable, painful decision and its existing workflow; identify data and authority constraints; choose a narrow task with a verifiable outcome; run a small pilot and compare against the current process. Ask the security and mission owners to define acceptable risk before launch.

TrustHow would you reduce hallucinations?

Start with a scoped task and high-quality sources. Retrieve relevant evidence, attach citations, expose freshness and uncertainty, and abstain when evidence is insufficient. Evaluate against representative questions and adversarial cases; keep a human in the loop where stakes require it. Citations alone do not prove a claim is supported.

EvaluationWhat would you measure in a pilot?

Define the task and baseline first. Track task completion, time to a verified answer, citation support, error severity, abstention quality, user adoption and accessibility. Segment by user and task; review failures with domain experts. A faster wrong answer is not a win.

SecurityWhat if a retrieved document contains malicious instructions?

Treat retrieved text as untrusted; keep it separate from system instructions, restrict tool permissions, validate actions outside the model, and test malicious documents. Monitor and contain failures. No prompt-only defense guarantees protection.

DeliveryHow do you balance speed and security?

Clarify the information category and decision risk, then scope a thin pilot with approved data and explicit guardrails. Work with security early, automate repeatable controls, agree on launch gates and a rollback plan. Speed comes from reducing uncertainty, not skipping accountability.

ScenarioA user says an answer is wrong. What next?

Capture the question, answer, source versions and impact without exposing unnecessary sensitive data. Reproduce, classify retrieval versus interpretation versus source failure, correct or withdraw the answer, notify affected owners where needed, and add a regression test. Measure recurrence.

BusinessHow would you build trust with a skeptical agency?

Listen to the user's actual task, show the evidence behind answers, explain limitations and data handling, and agree on evaluation criteria before a pilot. Bring procurement and security stakeholders in early; don't oversell certification or capabilities.

BehavioralTell me about navigating ambiguity.

Use situation → task → actions → result → reflection. Pick a real project, explain how you identified unknowns and made a reversible first decision, quantify the result if you can, and share what you'd change.

BehavioralTell me about a failure or disagreement.

Choose a genuine example. Explain your contribution without blaming others, how you surfaced evidence, what changed, and what you learned. A strong answer shows judgment and accountability, not a perfect record.

Trade-offWhen should an AI assistant say “I don't know”?

When sources are missing, stale, conflicting, outside scope, or do not support a consequential conclusion. Offer the best available evidence and a path to human review. Calibrate abstention against the cost of both false confidence and unnecessary refusals.

Turn the interview around

07

Good questions to ask them.

Pick the questions that match your interviewer. You don't need to ask all of them.

Mission & product

What problem is most urgent?

  • Who are your primary users, and what job are they trying to get done?
  • What does a successful deployment look like six months later?
  • Where does the product end and customer-specific work begin?
  • What have users taught you that changed your roadmap?
Team & role

How will I contribute?

  • What would success in this role look like after 30, 90, and 180 days?
  • How is the team structured, and who makes product decisions?
  • Which problems would I own versus support?
  • What do your strongest people do differently?
Trust & delivery

How do you ship responsibly?

  • How do you evaluate factual quality and source provenance?
  • How do security and privacy teams participate in product decisions?
  • What are the main deployment and authorization hurdles today?
  • How do you respond when a user finds an incorrect answer?
Business & diligence

What should I understand?

  • How do you balance customer-specific requests against a reusable product?
  • How do you distinguish pilots from sustained adoption?
  • How do government relations, procurement and the product team interact?
  • What can you share about company stage, funding, and runway?

Technical rehearsal / Hypothetical, not Coretsu architecture

11

Design a trustworthy policy brief.

Prompt: “A congressional staffer asks what changed in a policy since last month. Build an assistant that answers with evidence.” Rehearse this as a 20-minute design discussion.

ScopeUser, date range, jurisdiction, authority
RetrieveAllowlisted public APIs + versioned records
CompareNormalize, deduplicate, identify changes
ExplainCited claims + limits + human review
1 / Clarify the task before choosing a model

Ask whether “changed” means introduced text, enacted law, regulatory guidance, or implementation dates. Define jurisdictions and authoritative sources. Identify the cost of a missed change versus a false change. Start read-only; drafting a brief is not permission to send it or take an official action.

2 / Choose an architecture and explain the trade-offs

Use typed, allowlisted API tools for structured records; use text search or retrieval for supporting documents. Store source IDs, retrieval timestamps, effective dates, and document versions. Separate deterministic comparisons from model-generated explanations. A vector index helps semantic discovery but cannot by itself guarantee exact dates, completeness, or legal status.

Authenticate users and enforce any access rules before retrieval. Treat retrieved content as untrusted data. Constrain tool arguments and outbound endpoints; do not let a document instruct the assistant to fetch arbitrary URLs or expose user context.

3 / Handle failure without silently inventing certainty

When an API is unavailable, show coverage gaps and the last successful retrieval time. When sources disagree, expose both and prioritize the appropriate authority rather than blending them. If evidence is missing, abstain on that claim. Cache public source data by version, but avoid shared caching of sensitive user prompts or permission-dependent answers.

4 / Propose an evaluation and release gate

Build a reviewer-labeled set of time-bounded questions with authoritative source versions and expected changes. Measure change-detection recall, false-change rate, citation support per claim, date/status correctness, and abstention quality. Add API outages, amended documents, prompt injection, and cross-user isolation tests.

Compare with the current manual workflow for task completion and review time. Track p50/p95 latency and cost per completed, accepted brief—not just per generated response. Ship a limited pilot with reviewer approval, a rollback plan, and explicit quality thresholds agreed with the mission owner.

Five-minute self-review

Did you distinguish legal status from document similarity? Define a baseline? Show provenance? Handle partial retrieval? Explain what the model cannot decide? These are practice criteria, not claims about Coretsu's hiring rubric.

Choose your angle / Original practice material

12

Rehearse for your role.

The interview process is not public in the reviewed material. These are preparation tracks, not leaked or predicted interview questions.

Engineering / ML

Prove reliability, not just fluency

Practice the policy-brief design above, an API failure debugging story, and a model-change evaluation plan. Explain retrieval versus tool calling, tenant isolation, idempotent retries, and why citations can be valid links yet fail to support a claim.

Bring: one real architecture trade-off, a measured outcome, and a failure you helped diagnose.

Product / Design

Find the narrow valuable workflow

Map a staffer's task from question to verified output. Identify the user, buyer, security reviewer, and budget owner. Sketch how source freshness, conflicting evidence, and “not enough information” appear in the interface.

Bring: a discovery story and a pilot plan with success and stop criteria.

Deployment / Operations

Make adoption repeatable

Discuss onboarding, identity, data boundaries, training, support, incident response, and change management. Separate an enthusiastic demo from a maintained deployment with an accountable owner.

Bring: a rollout story, a risk register, and a 30/60/90-day plan to learn, pilot, and operationalize.

Commercial / Strategy

Understand the buying system

Qualify mission pain, procurement route, budget, evaluation criteria, and institutional sponsors. Explain why a lobbyist meeting, pilot, authorization milestone, and paid renewal are distinct signals.

Bring: a multi-stakeholder story and questions about repeatable revenue versus custom project work.

If you only have ten minutes

Read the 30-second company answer. Recheck FedRAMP status. Pick two real stories: ambiguity and ownership. Ask which workflow this role owns, how success is measured, and what currently blocks delivery. Never claim to know the internal stack.
