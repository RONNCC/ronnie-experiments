Cultural periodization

Everybody kept counting to three.

Coffee has a third wave. So do sociolinguistics, ska, chocolate, therapy, interface design, AI, democracy, and feminism. The phrase spread because it carries a claim: something was flattened, and a later generation went back for it.

This guide dates each label to the year it actually appeared, separates the ones scholars invented from the ones marketers borrowed, and hands the last word to the critics who think the metaphor hides most of the work.

How to read this

Every movement below is tagged with the status of its label. Some are institutional terms with a citable first use, such as Huntington's third wave of democratization in 1991 or Bødker's third wave HCI in 2006. Others are borrowed craft analogies, such as bean to bar chocolate echoing coffee, or fan periodizations, such as the disputed fifth wave of emo. Where the numbering breaks down, the card says so rather than smoothing it over.

A wave is a story a movement tells about what the last one flattened.

01

The four moves behind almost every numbered wave

Read enough of these histories and the same sequence shows up. Something solves a hard problem at scale. A second cohort turns it into culture. A third gets specific again. Then the number starts to fracture.

  1. Phase 1

    Solve a hard problem at scale

    Coffee in every kitchen. A ballot in every hand. A way to measure how a machine fails. Scale is the achievement, and the flattened thing is the cost.

  2. Phase 2

    Turn it into culture

    The espresso bar as a third place. The department store study as a method. The style record, the thought record, the federal statute.

  3. Phase 3

    Get specific again

    Single farm lots, speaker persona, intersectionality, work on how a person relates to a thought rather than whether it is true.

  4. Phase 4

    Fracture or leapfrog

    Either the tools get more technical, as with refractometers, or a scene skips the commercial peak to reach back two waves, as emo did after 2009.

05

When the wave metaphor lies

Ocean waves imply a single crest, then dead calm. Nancy Hewitt's 2010 collection No Permanent Waves points out what that hides: everyone who kept working in the troughs. Her alternative is radio, where several frequencies broadcast at once.
